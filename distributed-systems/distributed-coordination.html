Distributed Systems

Distributed Coordination

How Raft- and ZAB-backed stores expose watches, ephemeral membership, and leases so clusters agree on small, critical metadata without turning every app into a consensus implementer.

⏱ 14 min read

What it is

Distributed coordination is the infrastructure that lets many processes agree on small pieces of shared metadata—who is the leader, which nodes are alive, what configuration is current—without each application embedding its own consensus protocol. Production systems usually reach for a coordination service: Apache ZooKeeper (hierarchical znodes and watches), etcd (versioned key-value with leases and multi-key transactions), or HashiCorp Consul (Raft-backed KV plus optional service registry and health checks).

Martin Kleppmann frames the underlying requirement in Designing Data-Intensive Applications: when you need every replica to observe the same order of events, you need total order broadcast or an equivalent consensus-backed log. Coordination services ship that capability as an API—create/put, watch, session-bound ephemeral keys—so operators store kilobytes of critical state, not customer rows. Kubernetes is the familiar proof point: the control plane persists all cluster objects in etcd and reconciles desired state from that single linearisable source of truth.

Why it exists

Correct coordination—leader election without split brain, locks that actually exclude writers, membership that reflects real failures—reduces to consensus under crash faults. The FLP impossibility result shows you cannot build a perfect asynchronous consensus protocol; real systems add timing assumptions and accept bounded unavailability during partitions. Implementing Raft or ZAB yourself multiplies that risk: subtle bugs in membership changes, log truncation, or watch delivery show up only under production partitions.

Dedicated stores concentrate the hard parts behind a narrow contract. Kleppmann’s discussion of linearizability in DDIA applies directly: coordination metadata (epoch numbers, lock tokens, service registrations) is exactly the class of data where “read your latest write” and “everyone sees the same order” matter more than raw write throughput. Applications publish facts (“I am leader for term 7”, “config version 42”) and subscribe to changes; the service enforces ordering and durability.

The Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility result proves termination cannot be guaranteed in a fully asynchronous crash model; coordination services therefore embed partial synchrony—bounded election delays, lease TTLs, and operator expectations that networks eventually heal. That is why etcd may refuse writes during a majority partition rather than risk split brain, and why clients must handle watch reconnect loops after session expiry instead of assuming infinite availability.

When to use

  • Electing a single primary for databases, controllers, schedulers, or batch job runners where duplicate primaries would corrupt external state.
  • Distributed locks or semaphores when the guarded resource lacks compare-and-swap or transactional guards—typically paired with fencing tokens on the storage side.
  • Cluster membership and service registry metadata that must reflect process death quickly (ephemeral znodes, lease-bound keys).
  • Versioned configuration or feature flags that many instances should read consistently and react to via watches instead of polling.
  • Barrier, queue, and rendezvous recipes where all participants must observe the same transition (ZooKeeper recipes, etcd Txn patterns).

When not to use

  • Application or analytics data at megabyte values or terabyte totals—etcd’s guidance (~8 GB cluster data, ~1.5 MB per key) exists because consensus replication is the wrong price curve for bulk storage.
  • High-frequency data-plane writes (per-request counters, session caches, event streams)—throughput tops out in the low thousands of linearisable writes per second per cluster.
  • Problems solved with idempotent consumers, CRDTs, or gossip where eventual agreement is acceptable and stronger coordination would shrink availability.
  • Two-node “HA” without a tie-breaker—quorum needs an odd count (3, 5, 7); even pairs cannot survive symmetric partition safely.

Typical architecture

Clients treat the coordination cluster as a small, strongly consistent metadata plane; watches push notifications so workers react to leader changes or config updates without tight polling loops.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Linearisable metadata under crash faults—backed by Raft (etcd, Consul) or ZAB (ZooKeeper), matching the consistency model DDIA associates with coordination and locking.
  • Watch-driven notification removes hot polling paths; clients re-sync after reconnect with a fresh read plus re-registered watch.
  • Ephemeral znodes and leases tie keys to liveness—failed holders drop out of membership without manual cleanup.
  • Mature operational playbooks: etcd for Kubernetes, ZooKeeper’s decade of Kafka/HBase recipes, Consul for multi-DC service metadata.

Cons

  • Write scalability stops at consensus latency—one leader replicates each entry; expect hundreds to low thousands of linearisable writes per second, not millions.
  • Quorum sizing trades fault tolerance for blast radius: lose majority and the cluster stops accepting writes until operators recover or replace nodes.
  • Client complexity: one-shot watches, session timeouts, and retry storms during partitions are easy to get wrong in application code.
  • Operational cost: fast SSD WAL, odd-member clusters, backup/restore drills, and version upgrades are mandatory—not optional “day two” tasks.

Implementation notes

ZooKeeper models a filesystem of znodes with ACLs, sequential naming, and session-bound ephemerals. Official recipes document leader election, locks, queues, and barriers in terms of those primitives. ZAB provides total order on state changes; clients typically read from a quorum follower for scalability but use synchronous reads when they need the latest committed write. JVM clients must handle session expiration explicitly—any ephemeral node disappears when the session dies.

etcd exposes a flat key space with revision numbers, range watches, and gRPC streaming. Leases renew with keep-alive RPCs; associated keys vanish when TTL expires—ideal for locks and worker heartbeats. Multi-key Txn blocks implement compare-and-swap without race-prone read-modify-write loops. For Kubernetes clusters, prefer the built-in Lease and API machinery before colocating a second etcd solely for app metadata.

Consul layers DNS and HTTP service discovery on the same Raft log as its KV store. Sessions (TTL leases) back lock primitives; Connect adds mesh TLS. Choose Consul when you want discovery and coordination from one HA cluster; choose standalone etcd when Kubernetes or custom gRPC clients already standardise on etcd v3.

Guarantees versus assumptions: Kleppmann stresses that a lock is only as safe as the resource it protects—pair lease-based locks with monotonic fencing tokens on databases or object stores. Treat coordination latency as part of failover time: election plus watch propagation often spans seconds, not milliseconds.

Common failure modes

  • Missed watch windows: watches do not replay history; after reconnect, list or get the current state, then re-register the watch from the latest revision or zxid.
  • etcd I/O stalls: WAL fsync on slow or networked disks drives leader churn and etcdserver: request timed out errors—keep data dir on local NVMe.
  • Session or lease expiry while still working: long GC pauses or asymmetric partitions expire the session; the holder must stop mutating external systems—fencing rejects stale writers.
  • Keyspace bloat: unbounded unique keys (per-request IDs stored in etcd) inflate MVCC history until compaction and quotas break scheduling latency SLOs.
  • Split-brain at the application layer: two clients both believe they lead because only one side talks to quorum; always gate side effects on epoch or revision validated by storage.

Decision checklist

  • Is strong consistency required, or would an idempotent, eventually consistent design suffice?
  • On Kubernetes, can Leases, ConfigMaps, or the API server satisfy the use case without a separate ZooKeeper/etcd cluster?
  • Will values stay small and change infrequently—well inside etcd/ZooKeeper sizing guidance?
  • Do downstream systems enforce fencing tokens (or equivalent) so expired lock holders cannot corrupt data?
  • Are watch handlers idempotent and tested under kill -9, network partition, and rolling upgrades?
  • Is the coordination cluster odd-sized, on fast local disks, with monitored quorum health and restore drills?

Example use cases

  • Case study — Kubernetes persists Deployments, Pods, and Leases in etcd; controllers and the scheduler reconcile exclusively from that store (see the control plane overview).
  • Case study — Pre-KRaft Apache Kafka used ZooKeeper for broker registration, controller election, and topic partition leadership.
  • Case study — Apache HBase relies on ZooKeeper for master election and region assignment coordination.
  • Case study — HashiCorp Nomad elects its scheduler leader and tracks allocations through Consul’s Raft-backed state.

Related patterns

  • Consensus Algorithms — coordination services are operationalised Raft/ZAB; their limits are consensus limits.
  • Leader Election — ephemeral sequential znodes and etcd elections are the default implementation pattern.
  • Distributed Locking — leases and CAS transactions provide locks; fencing completes the safety story Kleppmann describes.
  • Service Discovery — Consul and etcd often hold both membership metadata and coordination keys in one cluster.
