Distributed Systems Consistency

Distributed Transactions

A distributed transaction coordinates commit or abort across resource managers via two-phase commit and XA—strong atomicity at the cost of blocking, coupling, and fragile coordinator recovery.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

A distributed transaction is a coordinated unit of work that either commits or aborts across multiple independent resource managers—relational databases, message brokers, or other XA-capable stores—as if it were one atomic operation. The usual implementation is two-phase commit (2PC): a transaction coordinator asks every participant to prepare (vote yes or no), then broadcasts a single commit or rollback decision once all votes are in. When every participant honors the protocol, you retain familiar ACID semantics across heterogeneous resources; when the coordinator or network fails at the wrong moment, participants can block in a prepared state holding locks until an operator or a recovered coordinator resolves the outcome.

Martin Kleppmann’s Designing Data-Intensive Applications treats distributed transactions as powerful but fragile: they couple failure domains, extend lock duration over WAN round trips, and do not compose cleanly across independently deployed services. That trade-off is why most microservice architectures prefer sagas and other application-level consistency patterns instead of spanning 2PC across team boundaries.

Why it exists

On a single machine, the database enforces atomicity with a local write-ahead log and lock manager. After you shard state across nodes—or split ownership across microservices—there is no shared log to roll back together. A crash between two local commits can leave one ledger updated and another untouched, which is unacceptable for settlement, inventory invariants, or paired publish-and-write operations. Distributed transactions answer that gap by externalizing the decision to a coordinator so all participants move forward or backward in lockstep, preserving the mental model of monolithic ACID even when data is physically distributed.

When to use

  • Participants live under one operational boundary and already expose XA or an equivalent prepare/commit interface through a shared transaction manager.
  • Regulatory or contractual rules require all-or-nothing updates across specific stores, and partial failure has legal or financial consequences.
  • The transaction touches a small, stable set of resource managers on a low-latency network where holding locks for multiple round trips is acceptable.
  • Operations staff can run, monitor, and recover a durable coordinator log with clear runbooks for in-doubt transactions.
  • You have verified end-to-end behavior under crash and partition scenarios—not only happy-path integration tests.

When not to use

  • Microservices each own a private database and release cadence; infrastructure-level XA ties availability and schema evolution across team boundaries.
  • Write throughput or user-facing latency budgets cannot tolerate locks held from prepare through commit across unreliable networks.
  • Some participants are managed services, serverless tiers, or document/key-value stores without reliable two-phase support.
  • You need partition tolerance without manual intervention—2PC is not a substitute for consensus, and a partitioned coordinator can block the whole flow.
  • A saga, outbox, or single-writer-per-aggregate design achieves adequate business consistency with less coupling.

The XA specification maps this protocol onto concrete resource managers through interfaces such as JTA in Java. A transaction manager drives prepare and commit, writes a durable coordinator log, and replays in-doubt branches after restart. Application servers typically embed managers such as Narayana or Atomikos; Spring’s JtaTransactionManager delegates to the same infrastructure.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Delivers atomic commit or rollback across heterogeneous stores without hand-written compensation for every branch.
  • Reuses familiar ACID vocabulary that operators and auditors already understand.
  • Mature enterprise databases and JMS-style brokers expose XA when all participants share a controlled deployment.
  • Central coordinator log provides a single place to inspect in-flight global transactions.

Cons

  • Coordinator failure during the commit window can leave participants blocked in PREPARED with locks held—the classic blocking problem Kleppmann documents in DDIA.
  • Lock duration stretches across multiple network round trips, which caps throughput on contended rows.
  • Three-phase commit variants reduce some blocking cases but add complexity and still fail under partitions.
  • Couples schema, uptime, and rollout of every participant in the global transaction.
  • Jepsen-style fault injection on real stacks often surfaces timeout, heuristic, and recovery edge cases that integration tests miss.

Implementation notes

Treat the coordinator log as part of your availability story: replicate it, back it up, and practice recovery of in-doubt transactions before production traffic depends on it. Participants need explicit XA timeouts aligned with client SLAs—factory defaults are often far longer than interactive callers tolerate. Heuristic outcomes (a participant committing or aborting alone after a timeout) should surface as critical alerts because they may diverge from the coordinator’s recorded decision.

Before adopting 2PC between services, compare against a saga with idempotent steps, a transactional outbox for reliable publish, or a single consensus group (Raft/etcd) that owns the coupled state. Google’s Spanner paper illustrates a different axis: externally consistent transactions backed by Paxos replication and a globally synchronized clock—not classic cross-database XA, but the same architectural question of when to pay for strong atomicity.

Chaos and partition testing (including analyses published by Jepsen / Kyle Kingsbury) are worth running against any stack that claims transparent distributed transactions.

Common failure modes

  • Coordinator loss after unanimous prepare: participants retain locks and wait for a coordinator that may not return promptly.
  • Aggressive participant timeout: a slow participant aborts while others commit, breaking global atomicity.
  • Heuristic commit or rollback: operators or libraries force a local outcome that does not match the coordinator log.
  • Partitioned coordinator: one subset receives COMMIT while others never leave PREPARED.
  • Lost coordinator log: in-doubt branches cannot be completed automatically and require manual reconciliation.

Decision checklist

  • Does every resource manager in scope implement prepare/commit reliably under your failure model?
  • Can you bound how long prepared locks may be held during coordinator outage?
  • Have you measured lock contention at peak transaction rates on shared rows?
  • Would a saga or outbox meet business rules with less cross-team coupling?
  • Is coordinator log durability, replication, and restore tested on a schedule?
  • Have you run partition or crash tests (chaos or Jepsen-style scenarios) on the full path?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A legacy settlement job updates two relational schemas in the same data centre through one JTA transaction manager so both ledgers commit or both roll back during end-of-day processing.
  • Illustrative scenario — An application server writes an order row and enlists a JMS session in the same global transaction so message publication cannot succeed unless the database commit does.
  • Case study — Mainframe CICS and IMS workloads have long used coordinated transaction entry to pair hierarchical databases with external updates; that history is why XA semantics remain entrenched in regulated batch estates.
  • Case study — Google Spanner provides globally ordered, externally consistent transactions via Paxos-backed shards and TrueTime—an alternative to stringing classical 2PC across unrelated operational databases.

Related patterns

  • Saga Pattern — sequences local commits with compensating rollbacks when cross-service 2PC is impractical.
  • Compensating Transactions — semantic undo steps that sagas invoke after partial forward progress.
  • Consensus Algorithms — replicate coordinator state safely when a single node would be a brittle 2PC bottleneck.
  • Distributed Locking — participants hold row or page locks through the prepared phase of 2PC.

Further reading

  • Martin Kleppmann — Designing Data-Intensive Applications — distributed transactions, exactly-once semantics, and why sagas are the common microservices escape hatch.
  • Two-Phase Commit — Martin Fowler — phase breakdown, blocking behavior, and coordinator responsibilities.
  • Saga — microservices.io (Chris Richardson) — the usual application-level alternative when XA cannot span services.
  • Spanner: Google’s Globally-Distributed Database — strong distributed transactions without classical cross-vendor 2PC.
  • Jepsen — empirical fault-injection analyses of databases and coordination products (Aphyr).
