Distributed Systems Essential

Saga Pattern

A saga sequences local commits across services and uses compensating rollbacks when a step fails—eventual business consistency without cross-service two-phase commit.

⏱ 12 min read

What it is

A saga is a long-running business process implemented as a sequence of local transactions, each confined to one service and database. If a later step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions over earlier steps in reverse order rather than rolling back through a single global two-phase commit. The pattern trades strict isolation and a single global snapshot for loose coupling, independent deployment, and progress without holding distributed locks across service boundaries.

Garcia-Molina and Salem introduced sagas in 1987 for long-lived database workflows; Chris Richardson and others adapted the idea for microservices when cross-service XA became impractical. Martin Kleppmann’s Designing Data-Intensive Applications contrasts this application-level consistency with distributed transactions that block participants while a coordinator decides.

Why it exists

Microservice architectures assign each bounded context its own datastore so teams can evolve schemas and availability independently. There is no shared transaction log spanning those stores, and infrastructure-level 2PC couples uptime, locking, and release trains across teams. Sagas preserve business-level consistency by committing forward locally and undoing semantically when a downstream step fails, which matches the failure and scaling assumptions described in the Dynamo paper’s emphasis on partition-tolerant, operator-driven recovery—without pretending one atomic transaction covers every service.

When to use

  • A business operation legitimately spans multiple services that each own their data and release cycle.
  • Workflow duration or human approvals make holding database locks across remote calls unacceptable.
  • Stakeholders accept eventual consistency and visible intermediate states between saga steps.
  • Every forward step has a designed, testable compensating action—or a documented pivot where compensation stops.
  • You can observe saga state, retries, and stuck compensations in production telemetry.

When not to use

  • Regulations or product rules require serialisable isolation across the whole operation as one atomic unit.
  • A step has no semantic inverse once it succeeds (irreversible shipment, irreversible notification, irreversible external transfer).
  • A single local transaction plus a transactional outbox already satisfies integration needs without orchestration overhead.
  • The organization cannot invest in idempotent handlers, durable saga state, and on-call playbooks for partial completion.

Typical architecture

In an orchestrated saga, a coordinator—often a state machine—issues commands, records progress durably, and triggers compensation when a step reports failure. In choreography, each service subscribes to domain events, performs its local transaction, and publishes the next event. No central module owns the script; the workflow is the composition of handlers. That minimizes coupling to a coordinator but shifts comprehension cost to documentation and distributed tracing.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Avoids distributed locks and prepared-state blocking across service databases.
  • Keeps each participant autonomous for schema, scaling, and failure isolation.
  • Fits long-running and human-in-the-loop processes that cannot hold rows locked indefinitely.
  • Orchestration mode centralizes visibility for support engineers and auditors.
  • Aligns with at-least-once messaging when every step is idempotent.

Cons

  • Does not provide global isolation—concurrent readers may observe partial saga outcomes.
  • Requires paired forward and compensating logic for every step, doubling design and test surface.
  • Choreography hides control flow inside many services, which complicates refactors and incident response.
  • Orchestrators risk becoming a “god service” if business rules accumulate without clear boundaries.
  • Partial failures demand the same operational rigor Kleppmann and Jepsen analyses recommend for any distributed workflow—assume retries and duplication.

Implementation notes

Persist saga correlation identifiers and current step in the same durable store that backs the orchestrator, or in an event-sourced aggregate, so restarts continue rather than fork duplicate workflows. Pair local commits with a transactional outbox (or change-data-capture publish) so integration events are not lost between database commit and broker send. Forward and compensating handlers should accept stable business keys and honor idempotency so at-least-once delivery is safe.

Study saga isolation anomalies from the original paper: concurrent sagas may exhibit lost updates, dirty reads, or fuzzy reads relative to one another. Mitigations include semantic locks, commutative updates, version checks, and reserving inventory before exposing payable totals. Choose orchestration when regulators or operators need one queryable state machine; choose choreography when teams already think in domain events end to end.

Durable workflow engines (Temporal, Camunda, AWS Step Functions) externalise state machines so process crashes do not lose the current step—prefer them over in-memory orchestrators when sagas run longer than a few seconds or require human approval. Set explicit timeouts per step and per saga; stuck compensations older than your business SLA should page on-call. Trace correlation identifiers must cross service boundaries the same way they do for synchronous RPC, because saga debugging is fundamentally distributed tracing work.

Common failure modes

  • Stuck compensation: a compensating call fails repeatedly and the saga halts mid-rollback without alerting.
  • Duplicate forward execution: broker redelivery or client retry runs a non-idempotent charge or reservation twice.
  • Interleaved sagas: two workflows update the same aggregate without versioning, silently dropping one outcome.
  • Ephemeral orchestrator state: process memory holds the current step, so crash loses knowledge of in-flight work.
  • Late pivot: compensation is attempted after a step that should have been marked non-reversible.

Decision checklist

  • Is every forward step paired with a tested compensating action or an explicit pivot?
  • Do handlers deduplicate on saga id, business key, or idempotency token?
  • Does orchestrator state survive process restart without manual repair?
  • Are publishes tied to local commits via outbox, CDC, or an equivalent pattern?
  • Have product and compliance stakeholders accepted intermediate states and eventual alignment?
  • Can on-call engineers trace one saga id across services in your observability stack?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A checkout saga creates an order locally, captures payment, reserves stock, and schedules fulfillment; a stock failure triggers refund and cancel steps in reverse order.
  • Illustrative scenario — A travel-booking workflow reserves flight, hotel, and rental car in separate services and compensates earlier reservations when a downstream supplier rejects a hold.
  • Case study — Chris Richardson’s microservices.io saga pattern catalog documents orchestration and choreography approaches widely adopted in microservice training and architecture reviews.
  • Case study — Garcia-Molina and Salem’s SIGMOD 1987 sagas paper formalized compensating transactions and isolation anomalies that still guide saga design today.

Related patterns

  • Distributed Transactions — global 2PC when participants share infrastructure and strict atomicity justifies blocking.
  • Compensating Transactions — semantic undo steps invoked during saga rollback.
  • Transactional Outbox — reliable event publication after each local saga step commits.
  • Event Sourcing — event log as saga audit trail and choreography transport.
