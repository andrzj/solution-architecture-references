Distributed Systems Coordination

Leader Election

Safely designating exactly one coordinator in a cluster — Raft terms and majority votes, consensus-backed coordination stores, lease-based promotion, and fencing tokens when TTL alone is not enough.

⏱ 12 min read

What it is

Leader election is the process by which a distributed group picks one member as the authoritative coordinator for a period—handling writes, assigning partitions, running control loops, or holding a cluster-wide lock. Followers observe the leader and trigger a new election when the leader stops or appears unreachable. Martin Kleppmann’s Designing Data-Intensive Applications (DDIA) treats leader-based replication as the default way strong databases and logs achieve a single serial order; election is how that leadership role is granted and revoked safely.

Election is not merely “who has the lock”: it must run under partitions and crashes so that two nodes do not both believe they are leader, or so that a stale leader cannot corrupt shared state. Raft bakes election into consensus via monotonic terms and majority votes; ZooKeeper and etcd expose election recipes on top of replicated logs; simpler lease-based schemes trade operational ease for weaker guarantees unless paired with fencing tokens at the resources being guarded.

Why it exists

Many coordination problems—replicated logs, metadata stores, job schedulers, partition leadership—have a natural single-writer shape. Delegating decisions to one leader cuts cross-node chatter from pairwise negotiation to linear fan-in (clients → leader → followers). The hard part is fault tolerance: any timeout-based “the leader is dead” guess can be wrong under slow GC, long I/O, or partial network loss, which is why production designs lean on majority quorums (as in Raft’s RequestVote RPCs) or on consensus-backed stores rather than ad hoc heartbeats alone.

When to use

  • You need a single writer or serializer for a replicated log, database primary, or metadata service (DDIA’s leader-based replication model).
  • Partitioned systems (streaming, sharded storage) require one elected leader per shard to order operations for that shard.
  • Control-plane components (Kubernetes controllers, cluster schedulers) must run reconciliation on exactly one active instance.
  • Distributed cron or background sweeps must not double-run across horizontally scaled app instances.
  • You can accept brief unavailability during failover rather than risking divergent primaries.

When not to use

  • Leaderless replication with tunable quorums (Dynamo-style) meets availability and consistency goals without a permanent elected primary.
  • Conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) or gossip convergence remove the need for a central coordinator.
  • Write throughput must scale out within one logical dataset—a single leader caps horizontal write scaling for that group.
  • Election adds latency and infrastructure (etcd/ZooKeeper) that outweigh benefits for embarrassingly parallel workloads.

Typical architecture

Raft’s election sub-protocol is the reference design for safe promotion: followers become candidates when they miss heartbeats; the candidate increments its term and requests votes from a majority; the winner becomes leader for that term and begins append-only replication. Consensus stores (etcd, Consul) implement this internally; application code typically watches keys or leases instead of speaking Raft directly.

An alternate pattern uses coordination-service recipes: candidates create ephemeral sequential znodes; the lowest sequence number is leader; others watch their predecessor. On session loss the ephemeral node vanishes and the watch fires. This is how many pre-Raft stacks ran Kafka controller election and similar control metadata—today often replaced by KRaft’s internal Raft log or etcd-backed operators.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Establishes a single serialization point per shard or cluster—clients and followers know where to send writes.
  • Consensus-backed election (Raft terms, ZAB epochs) ties leadership to replicated logs so promotion and replication advance together.
  • Off-the-shelf recipes on etcd, Consul, and ZooKeeper avoid home-grown failure detectors for most control-plane workloads.
  • Fencing tokens and lease APIs let downstream storage reject stale leaders without trusting network heartbeats alone.

Cons

  • Write throughput for one logical partition caps at one leader’s capacity until you shard leadership.
  • Failover windows—election, log catch-up, client redirect—add seconds of write unavailability unless you dual-route reads carefully.
  • TTL-only leadership without fencing invites zombie writers after GC pauses or partitions (Kleppmann’s critique).
  • Ring-election heuristics (bully, ring) trade simplicity for chatty O(n²) traffic unsuited to large fleets.

Implementation notes

Prefer consensus-backed election when correctness matters: Kubernetes controllers use the Lease API backed by etcd’s Raft log; Consul and etcd expose session/lock primitives with linearisable semantics. DDIA emphasises that election safety and replication ordering are two facets of the same consensus problem—implementing one without the other invites subtle bugs.

Raft tuning: election timeouts must exceed typical round-trip time and GC pauses but stay short enough for tolerable failover. Randomise timeouts (150–300 ms is a common starting band on LAN) to avoid split votes. Jepsen’s analyses of etcd, Consul, and other Raft stacks repeatedly surface mis-tuned timeouts and membership changes as sources of leadership flapping—not algorithm failure in the abstract, but operational edge cases worth load-testing.

Fencing tokens (DDIA Ch. 8–9; Kleppmann’s distributed locking essay) close the gap when a zombie leader outlives its lease. Each election bumps a monotonic token—Raft term, Kafka leader epoch, or a lock-service counter. Downstream storage (object store, block device, relational primary) must reject operations whose token is lower than the maximum already observed, even if the client still believes it is leader.

TTL leases and heartbeats (Redis SET NX PX, database advisory leases, Kubernetes Lease renewals) are acceptable for efficiency leadership—deduplicating cron, throttling work—when duplicate execution is wasteful but not corrupting. For correctness leadership over mutable shared state, leases alone are insufficient without fencing or majority quorum on the write path.

Observability: expose current term/epoch, last successful heartbeat, and leader identity to metrics and logs. Alert on rapid leadership churn; correlate with network partitions and deployment events.

Common failure modes

  • Stale leader on minority partition: the old primary keeps serving until storage fencing or client quorum checks reject it—otherwise divergent logs accumulate.
  • Split vote loops: symmetric election timeouts in Raft leave a term without a winner until randomized backoff breaks the tie.
  • Premature step-down: a single dropped heartbeat triggers election while the leader is still healthy, causing needless leadership churn.
  • Quorum loss: too many replicas down at once; no candidate can gather majority votes and the control plane freezes.
  • Unsafe membership change: overlapping majorities during voter reconfiguration admit two leaders with valid-looking terms.
  • Lease without token check: Redis/etcd lease expires during a long STW GC; the holder resumes work unaware a peer took over.

Decision checklist

  • Is leadership per shard or global—and does each shard have an independent election path?
  • Does correctness demand consensus election, or is an efficiency lease acceptable if duplicates are idempotent?
  • Do guarded resources enforce monotonic fencing tokens (terms, epochs) on every mutating call?
  • Are heartbeat and election timeouts validated against measured WAN RTT and JVM pause p99?
  • Is there an operator runbook for quorum loss and forced step-down without manual split-brain?
  • Do metrics and logs expose current leader identity, term, and election rate with alerts on flapping?

Example use cases

  • Case study — Apache Kafka KRaft mode elects a controller and per-partition leaders via an internal Raft metadata quorum; broker leader epochs fence stale brokers after failover.
  • Case study — Kubernetes control-plane components (controller-manager, scheduler, cloud-controller-manager) use Lease objects in etcd so only one replica actively reconciles cluster state.
  • Case study — Patroni promotes a PostgreSQL standby to primary after detecting leader failure, coordinating through DCS quorum (etcd, Consul, or ZooKeeper).
  • Illustrative scenario — A horizontally scaled billing service runs nightly aggregation on whichever instance wins an etcd session-based election; duplicates would inflate metrics but not corrupt ledger rows, so leases suffice without storage fencing.
