Consensus Algorithms

Consensus algorithms replicate an ordered log despite crashes and partitions; Raft and Paxos underpin etcd, Consul, and strongly consistent coordination metadata.

⏱ 14 min read

What it is

Distributed consensus is the problem of getting a set of replicas to agree on a single value or on an append-only log order despite crash failures and unreliable networks. A consensus protocol typically guarantees agreement (no two correct nodes decide differently), validity (only proposed values are chosen), and termination (correct nodes eventually decide) under explicit system assumptions. In production, consensus backs replicated state machines—configuration stores, shard metadata, leader records, and lock services—so clients see one coherent history even when individual machines restart.

Raft (Ongaro and Ousterhout) and Paxos (Lamport) are the dominant crash-fault-tolerant families; both rely on quorums so committed entries survive minority loss. Martin Kleppmann’s Designing Data-Intensive Applications connects consensus to linearisable reads and writes, while the Dynamo and Jepsen literature show what happens when teams skip it on the data path.

Why it exists

Independent replicas that accept writes without coordination can diverge permanently after partitions or crashes, which breaks invariants for locks, leases, and financial metadata. Consensus provides a safe replication substrate: one leader (in leader-based protocols) orders client updates, replicates them to followers, and commits after a quorum acknowledges.

The Fischer–Lynch–Paterson (FLP) impossibility result is the formal reason consensus feels hard: in an asynchronous network where messages can be delayed without bound and processes may crash, no deterministic protocol guarantees that every correct process eventually decides. That does not forbid useful systems—it explains why production protocols assume partial synchrony (election timeouts that eventually fire, or failure detectors that become accurate after a quiet period). Raft’s randomized election timeouts and Paxos’s eventual leader stabilization are engineering responses to FLP, not contradictions of it. Operators who shorten timeouts below RTT still “solve” liveness on paper while destabilizing leaders in practice.

When to use

  • You need a strongly consistent replicated log for metadata, configuration, or coordination—not the primary high-volume data plane.
  • Leader election, distributed locks, or membership must not admit split-brain without fencing.
  • Cluster size stays small enough that quorum RPC overhead is acceptable on your latency budget.
  • You can operate odd-sized quorums, persistent logs, and rehearsed recovery procedures.
  • Crash faults are the threat model; Byzantine actors require different protocols and larger quorums.

When not to use

  • The hot data path needs massive write throughput—consensus serializes through a leader and pays at least one round trip to a majority.
  • Wide-area latency dominates and product rules allow eventual consistency (Dynamo-style AP designs).
  • Every write would require a new consensus group—prefer hierarchical metadata plus partition-local availability.
  • You have not budgeted for Jepsen-class testing of your chosen implementation under partition and clock skew.

Cluster sizing is a consensus decision, not folklore. A Raft group of three voters tolerates one simultaneous crash or partition minority; five voters tolerate two failures while still requiring a majority of three for commit. Even numbers (four voters) do not increase useful fault tolerance—you still need three acknowledgements, so the fourth member adds cost without an extra failure domain. Witness or observer nodes can participate in elections without storing full logs when tie-breaking matters more than replica count on data nodes.

Embedding etcd or Consul buys a tested Raft implementation, but operators still own timeout math. Election and heartbeat intervals are commonly hundreds of milliseconds on a LAN and one to two seconds across regions; setting them below measured round-trip time causes leader flapping, while setting them too high stretches failover after real crashes. Expect roughly hundreds to low thousands of linearisable writes per second per small cluster—enough for metadata, not for application hot paths.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Delivers a total order on committed entries, enabling linearisable coordination APIs.
  • Survives minority replica loss when majorities overlap across configuration changes.
  • Raft’s specification is teaching-friendly; raft.github.io publishes the paper, visualizations, and reference implementations.
  • Mature open-source embeds (etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV) reduce the need to roll your own.

Cons

  • Write latency includes replication to a quorum; leaders do not scale writes horizontally within one group.
  • Paxos-family protocols are subtle to implement completely—production bugs often appear only under partition tests.
  • Crash-fault Raft/Paxos do not defend against malicious replicas; BFT protocols pay higher quorum and message costs.
  • Misconfigured membership changes or stale leaders can still harm availability until operators intervene.

Implementation notes

Raft log matching and commit: each log entry carries a term and index. The leader replicates with AppendEntries, maintaining per-follower nextIndex and matchIndex. If followers reject a prefix, the leader decrements and retries until the follower accepts a shared suffix—this is the log-matching property that prevents divergent committed histories. A leader commits an entry for application only when it is stored on a majority of that leader’s current term (Raft’s commit rule prevents committing stale-term entries that could be overwritten after election). Followers learn committed entries when the leader advances commitIndex in subsequent heartbeats.

Paxos vs Raft vs variants: Lamport’s single-decree Paxos proves agreement on one value; Multi-Paxos stitches repeated prepare/accept rounds into a replicated log with a stable leader for throughput. Production Multi-Paxos (Chubby, early Spanner shards) leaves many recovery details to implementers—Raft’s paper consolidated election, replication, membership, and snapshots into one teachable spec. EPaxos (leaderless consensus) lets different commands commit through intersecting quorums without a single serializer—higher coordination complexity, useful when WAN leaders hurt but command sets are mostly commutative. ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast (ZAB) is ZooKeeper’s atomic broadcast protocol—Raft-like in operation for operators of Kafka-adjacent metadata stores, though the specification differs from Raft’s wording.

Membership changes: adding or removing voters naively can admit two overlapping majorities across configuration boundaries—the classic route to split brain. Raft’s joint consensus transitional configuration requires quorums that intersect old and new voter sets during the change; etcd applies one-server-at-a-time operational procedure derived from the same safety argument. Skipping joint consensus in home-grown systems is a frequent Jepsen failure theme.

Operational embeds: Kubernetes stores cluster state in etcd (Raft + WAL). HashiCorp Consul exposes key-value and session primitives on Raft. Apache ZooKeeper’s ZAB backs Kafka metadata controllers and similar control planes. Google Spanner shards data across Paxos groups and layers SQL and TrueTime on top—consensus at shard granularity rather than one global group.

Testing: Before trusting any coordination layer, review independent fault-injection results. Jepsen publishes partition-heavy histories for many databases and coordination products. Tune election timeouts above measured WAN RTT and JVM pause budgets; enable quorum-checked reads when stale leader serves would violate product guarantees.

Common failure modes

  • Leader flapping: marginal networks trigger repeated elections; workloads stall without careful timeout tuning.
  • Split vote: competing candidates tie in a term until randomized backoff breaks symmetry.
  • Stale leader writes: partitioned leader accepts client traffic until fencing or quorum checks reject it.
  • Unmerged log tails: recovered nodes must truncate divergent suffixes per log-matching rules before serving.
  • Unsafe reconfiguration: overlapping majorities during membership change can admit two leaders.

Decision checklist

  • Does the use case require linearisable history, or is eventual consistency enough?
  • Is replica count odd with overlapping majorities across planned changes?
  • Are heartbeat and election timeouts validated against production RTT and pause profiles?
  • Is the failure model crash-only rather than Byzantine?
  • Are you embedding etcd, Consul, or another maintained implementation instead of writing consensus?
  • Have you reviewed Jepsen (or equivalent) results for the exact version you deploy?

  • Case study — Google Spanner uses Paxos per shard with synchronized clocks for externally consistent transactions across a global fleet.

  • Distributed Locking — safest locks sit atop consensus stores with fencing.
  • Distributed Coordination — etcd, Consul, and ZooKeeper package consensus as a service.
  • Quorum Patterns — majority overlap that makes committed entries durable.

Further reading

  • Paxos Made Simple — Leslie Lamport — entry point to Multi-Paxos and replicated-state-machine use.
  • Martin Kleppmann — Designing Data-Intensive Applications — consensus, replication, and consistency models.
  • Spanner: Google’s Globally-Distributed Database — Paxos-backed shards and strong distributed transactions.
  • Dynamo: Amazon’s Highly Available Key-value Store — contrast case when the data path favors availability over consensus strong ordering.
  • Jepsen — partition-oriented correctness analyses of databases and coordination systems (Aphyr).
  • Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process — Fischer, Lynch & Paterson — the FLP theorem.
  • EPaxos: Generalizing Paxos Through Democratic Replication — Moraru, Andersen & Kaminsky.
