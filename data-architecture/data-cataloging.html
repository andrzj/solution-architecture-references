Data Architecture

Data Cataloging

A centralized inventory of data assets across the organization — capturing technical metadata, business context, data lineage, ownership, and classification to enable discovery, governance, and trust.

⏱ 9 min read

What it is

A data catalog is the organization’s searchable map of datasets, pipelines, dashboards, and models—linking physical locations to business meaning, ownership, sensitivity, and lineage. Good catalogs answer “does this table already exist?”, “who owns it?”, and “what breaks if I rename this column?” without weeks of tribal archaeology.

They combine automated harvesting (warehouse schemas, dbt manifests, orchestrator metadata) with stewardship (descriptions, glossary terms, classifications). Open platforms include DataHub, Amundsen, and Apache Atlas; commercial and cloud-native options include Alation, Collibra, Atlan, AWS Glue, Google Dataplex, and Microsoft Purview.

In Dehghani’s Data Mesh, the catalog is a core piece of the self-serve data platform: domain teams publish discoverable data products, while federated computational governance applies standards through shared metadata services rather than a central data team gate. Contracts specify behavior; the catalog records what is available and how it connects—see Dehghani on moving beyond the monolithic lake and Fowler’s principles overview.

Mature inventory entries combine four reinforcing metadata layers. Technical metadata captures schemas, column statistics, and optional samples; business metadata links glossary definitions and plain-language descriptions to physical fields; operational metadata records row counts, job status, and measured freshness; governance metadata carries stewards, sensitivity labels, and retention hints. Discovery UX improves when warehouse query logs rank frequently joined tables alongside steward-curated descriptions, so newcomers see both popularity signals and authoritative narrative.

Why it exists

Data volume outpaced institutional memory. Warehouses, lakes, SaaS replicas, and streams multiply; without a graph of assets, engineers duplicate tables, analysts trust the wrong “official” revenue definition, and privacy teams cannot scope erasure requests. Catalogs reduce search friction and give compliance a substrate for classification and lineage.

Opacity costs compound quietly. Competing definitions of “active customer” or “net revenue” survive in parallel marts; deletion requests stall because nobody can enumerate derived SaaS exports; model trainers bind to staging tables that never earned production SLAs. A catalog does not manufacture truth—it makes declared owners, lineage, and quality signals visible so drift is negotiable instead of invisible.

They do not replace engineering judgment—stewards must still curate—but they convert one-off expertise into durable metadata that onboarding, impact analysis, and mesh-style product publishing can reuse.

Harvest-only catalogs without stewardship rot within quarters—assign minimum description coverage targets per domain and block “certified” badges until quality tests pass, not when a manager clicks approve.

When to use

  • When analysts or engineers routinely lose time finding authoritative datasets across more than a handful of platforms.
  • When GDPR, HIPAA, or similar regimes require knowing where personal data lives and which pipelines propagate it.
  • When mesh or product-oriented data strategies need a neutral discovery layer for domain-published datasets.
  • When ML governance must tie models to training tables, features, and freshness guarantees.
  • When schema or deprecation work needs column-level lineage to enumerate downstream dashboards and jobs.
  • When finance, product, and privacy leads need a shared vocabulary before executive metric debates, and tribal definitions in slide decks are no longer acceptable.
  • When self-serve platforms must show who to page when freshness badges turn red without routing every question through a central data team.

When not to use

  • Do not invest in enterprise catalog software without named stewards—empty descriptions erode trust faster than no catalog at all.
  • Do not treat discovery metadata as enforcement; access control and contracts still own authorization and behavioral guarantees.
  • Do not expect crawlers alone to infer business meaning; glossary curation remains a human responsibility.
  • Do not catalog everything day one—prioritize high-traffic assets while pipelines and naming stabilize.
  • Do not treat BI tool native lineage as a full catalog replacement when column-level impact analysis across warehouses and exports still matters.

Typical architecture

Connectors ingest schemas, query logs, and pipeline manifests into a metadata graph. Search and lineage APIs feed analyst UIs and engineering CLIs. Stewards enrich business terms; policy engines read classification tags to drive column masking or export rules. Mesh deployments attach product owners, contract links, and SLO badges to each entry.

Downstream systems consume the same graph: dynamic masking rules in the warehouse react to column tags, export scanners reject extracts that omit classification, and deployment pipelines fail merges that reference undocumented sensitive fields. IDE plugins and notebook extensions query catalog APIs for inline ownership, reducing context switches between orchestration repos and stewardship wikis.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Shortens discovery from ad hoc Slack threads to faceted search with usage signals and steward contacts.
  • Surfaces lineage for impact analysis before destructive schema changes ship.
  • Centralizes glossary terms so “customer” and “MRR” align across finance and product analytics.
  • Feeds compliance workflows with PII tags tied to physical columns across heterogeneous stores.
  • Supports mesh publishing—products remain discoverable without a single monolithic warehouse team.
  • Embeds trust signals—usage rank, assertion history, steward contacts—next to search results instead of in separate runbooks.

Cons

  • Demands continuous stewardship; neglected catalogs become misleading inventories.
  • Lineage gaps appear for ad hoc SQL, notebooks, and bespoke ETL unless instrumented deliberately.
  • Connector sprawl multiplies as each new SaaS or engine needs maintenance.
  • Automated PII classification produces false positives and negatives without sampling review.
  • Adoption stalls unless search is embedded in existing BI, notebook, and IDE entry points.
  • Steward bandwidth becomes a bottleneck when every new SaaS replica expects manual descriptions before automated harvest catches up.

Implementation notes

Prioritize automated ingestion: warehouse introspection, dbt manifest.json lineage, and orchestrator task metadata deliver quick wins before mandating manual documentation. Extend to query-log popularity so search ranks assets people actually use.

Assign domain stewards aligned to mesh products or subject areas; tie their OKRs to description coverage and glossary links. Define a compact classification taxonomy (public, internal, confidential, PII, quasi-PII) and wire tags to access policies—not slide decks alone. Store classifications in version control beside schema migrations when regulations require auditable history, not only UI edits.

Pair regex detectors for email, payment-card, and government-id patterns with steward sampling for ambiguous column names. Treat GDPR or HIPAA readiness as integration work: tags must reach RBAC, masking engines, and erasure runbooks—not remain decorative badges on table pages.

Publish contract status, freshness, and quality assertion results on catalog pages so consumers assess trust without opening Jira. Run adoption programs—office hours, embedded search in BI and notebooks, lightweight gamification for description coverage—so discovery becomes habit. Dehghani’s platform tenet expects self-serve discovery with federated standards; the catalog is where those standards become visible metadata rather than oral tradition.

DAMA-aligned metadata: DAMA-DMBOK distinguishes data glossary, lineage, and stewardship roles—catalog entries should bind business terms to physical assets with named stewards, not anonymous “data team” ownership. Automated harvesters ingest schema from Snowflake, Glue, or Postgres but still need humans to classify sensitivity and document intended use.

Detection: rising “unknown dataset” usage in query logs often means analysts bypassed the catalog; falling description coverage percentage after layoffs signals rot. Pair Popularity metrics with certified-badge workflows so certification means tested quality, not a one-time tag.

Lineage and classification: OpenLineage or native engine lineage should flow into the catalog so impact analysis answers which dashboards break when a silver table pauses. PII tags need steward approval workflows—automated regex classifiers propose labels, humans certify before wide RBAC opens. Without classification, mesh-style self-serve becomes self-serve leakage.

Operating model: domain teams publish datasets; platform supplies connectors, SLAs on harvest freshness, and training. Measure time-to-discover for new hires as an adoption KPI. A catalog nobody searches within thirty days of onboarding is inventory, not governance infrastructure.

Catalog UI search quality matters as much as metadata completeness—stemming on business terms, filters by domain, and ranking by certified plus recent usage beat alphabetical table lists inherited from metastore dumps.

Quarterly audits should sample ten “certified” datasets and verify descriptions, owners, and freshness tests still match production behavior. Stale certification without re-test is a common audit finding.

Common failure modes

  • Launch without owners: Crawlers populate thousands of tables; every description stays blank; users revert to spreadsheets.
  • Lineage holes: dbt is covered but notebook transforms are invisible, so impact analysis misses critical dashboards.
  • Glossary forks: Competing definitions for the same term persist because no steward reconciles them.
  • Big-bang scope: Multi-year catalog projects finish with half the metadata already stale.
  • Tagging without enforcement: PII columns are labeled but warehouse RBAC ignores classification, so labels provide false assurance.
  • Stale popularity signals: Query-log ranking still boosts a deprecated mart months after migration because refresh jobs lag, sending analysts to the wrong “official” table.

Decision checklist

  • Are stewards named per domain or product with time allocated for curation?
  • Are the top twenty datasets by query volume fully documented and glossary-linked?
  • Do connectors cover the primary warehouse, lake, dbt, and orchestration stack?
  • Is classification integrated with access control or masking policies?
  • Do catalog pages expose contract version, owner, and last quality check?
  • Is catalog search reachable from analyst and engineer default workflows?
  • Do erasure and legal-hold playbooks query classification and lineage APIs instead of manual spreadsheets?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — Privacy receives a deletion request; PII-tagged columns and downstream lineage list every warehouse and export table to scrub within the statutory window.
  • Illustrative scenario — A new analyst searches “net revenue,” lands on the canonical glossary entry, and jumps to the gold dbt model with steward contact and freshness badge.
  • Illustrative scenario — Before renaming a shared column, engineering exports downstream lineage covering Looker explores and Airflow DAGs to schedule coordinated releases.
  • Illustrative scenario — A platform team wires catalog search into the corporate notebook default kernel so every new Spark session suggests gold tables with freshness badges before analysts paste table names from memory.
  • Case study — Lyft’s Amundsen and LinkedIn’s DataHub influenced modern discovery-first catalogs; mesh adopters pair them with domain-owned products per Dehghani’s writing.
