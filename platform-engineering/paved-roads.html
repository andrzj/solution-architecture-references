Platform Engineering Pattern

Paved Roads

The supported toolchain and operational contract your platform team maintains—so teams on the road ship fastest with embedded security, GitOps on Kubernetes, and approved Crossplane compositions, while off-road work stays visible and bounded.

⏱ 11 min read

What it is

A paved road is the platform team’s commitment to fully support an opinionated set of tools, runtimes, and operational practices so that teams who stay on the road get the fastest delivery and the strongest guarantees (security, compliance, on-call integration, cost visibility). The road is not a single template: it is the curated ecosystem—Kubernetes as the compute plane, GitOps for deploys, approved observability stacks, and platform-maintained integrations—within which golden paths and self-service workflows operate.

Paved-road thinking distinguishes supported defaults from mandates. Teams may leave the road (“go off-road”) when they have legitimate specialised needs, but they accept reduced platform support, manual compliance evidence, and responsibility for drift. Netflix’s “paved road” framing and Spotify’s “golden path” culture both emphasise that the supported path must be genuinely excellent—otherwise adoption is performative and shadow tooling appears.

On the road, paved-road templates are the executable face of policy: Backstage Software Templates and cookiecutter-style scaffolds for each supported stack, reusable GitHub Actions or GitLab CI includes that cannot be stripped without breaking the pipeline, and infra-as-code modules (Terraform, Crossplane compositions, Helm umbrella charts) that only expose enum-safe parameters. A template is “on-road” when its output automatically inherits road tags in the catalog, lands in an approved namespace tier, and cannot merge without mandated scan stages. Templates that merely suggest best practice but allow silent bypass are documentation, not pavement.

Why it exists

Without a paved road, every team negotiates its own combination of cluster access, CI vendor, secret store, and logging agent. Platform engineers cannot reasonably on-call for hundreds of snowflake stacks; security cannot attest to controls that differ per repository; and new hires face a different “production story” on every team.

Centralising support on a paved road concentrates platform investment: one GitOps pattern (Argo CD or Flux), one ingress and certificate story, one set of Crossplane compositions for databases and buckets, and one service catalog contract. Improvements roll out once and benefit every team still on the road—fixing a CVE in a shared base image or tightening a Kyverno policy scales organisation-wide.

Adoption metrics close the loop between product management and platform engineering. Executives rarely fund abstraction without evidence; tie the road to numbers such as the share of production components with road: standard labels, median hours from template run to first successful production deploy, quarterly reduction in undifferentiated platform tickets per hundred engineers, and the count of active off-road exceptions with aged review dates. When those metrics plateau, the problem is usually road quality—slow templates, missing stacks, or flaky shared CI—not developer stubbornness.

When to use

  • Organisations with 50+ engineers and multiple product teams that need predictable production posture without reviewing every toolchain choice ad hoc.
  • When Kubernetes is the shared runtime and you want a single control plane for apps, platform CRDs, and Crossplane claims.
  • When compliance or regulated industries require demonstrable standard controls on the majority of workloads.
  • When platform team capacity is limited—explicit road boundaries clarify what is supported vs best-effort.
  • When measuring platform ROI through adoption metrics (% workloads on road, ticket reduction, time-to-first-deploy).
  • When you can publish at least two maintained paved-road templates per primary language (service API, async worker, scheduled job) with semver and changelog.
  • When FinOps and security stakeholders need one dashboard roll-up of on-road vs off-road spend and control coverage.

When not to use

  • Early-stage teams still searching for product–market fit where experimentation speed outweighs standardisation—use lightweight guidelines instead of a formal road map.
  • When the paved road is declared before the platform can keep it current (stale runtimes, broken templates)—a bad road drives off-road migration faster than no road.
  • When leadership treats the road as mandatory with no escape hatch—niche workloads (real-time gaming, embedded, heavy batch ML) will resist or route around platform.
  • Single-team monolith shops where one repo and one pipeline already imply de facto standardisation.

Typical architecture

Conceptually the road layers supported capabilities; golden paths and self-service sit on top as concrete entry points.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Clear support contract: platform on-call, runbooks, and SLAs apply to on-road workloads.
  • Security and reliability controls are economy-of-scale—patch once in a cluster addon or composition schema.
  • Faster onboarding: new engineers learn one deployment model and one catalog metadata shape.
  • FinOps and tagging policies enforced uniformly via Crossplane and admission policy.
  • Leadership can steer investment toward road gaps with adoption data instead of anecdote.
  • Template semver lets platform teams ship road upgrades without silent drift across hundreds of repos.
  • Adoption scorecards make trade-offs visible: invest in the next template or shrink exception backlog.

Cons

  • Road definition is political—teams disagree on what belongs on the road vs optional.
  • Off-road services still exist; someone must govern exceptions and periodic re-certification.
  • Kubernetes + Crossplane operations are non-trivial; a weak platform team makes the road fragile.
  • Risk of stagnation if the road isn’t versioned and sunset policies for legacy patterns are missing.
  • Over-broad road tries to cover every stack and becomes lowest-common-denominator.

Implementation notes

Publish a written road map: supported language runtimes, required CI gates, cluster tiers, and Crossplane claim types. Version it (v2025-Q1) and link from the developer portal landing page. Mark each component with lifecycle: experimental, supported, deprecated.

Use the service catalog to tag components road: standard vs road: exception. Golden path templates should default new services to standard. Off-road registration captures owner, justification, review date, and compensating controls (extra scanning, restricted blast radius).

On Kubernetes, encode the road in admission policy: workloads on road namespaces must use approved service accounts, read-only root filesystem where applicable, and pull from approved registries. Crossplane compositions should expose only safe parameter surfaces (instance size enums, environment labels)—not raw cloud API passthrough.

Measure quarterly: percentage of production workloads on road, mean time to approve off-road requests, and top reasons for exceptions. Feed results into platform backlog.

Template catalogue design: Organise paved-road templates in three tiers so expectations stay clear. Tier A (mandatory road) covers net-new customer-facing services—scaffold, CI, GitOps app, default dashboards, and PagerDuty hooks are non-optional. Tier B (recommended road) covers internal tools and batch jobs with softer defaults but the same deploy rail. Tier C lists experimental scaffolds the platform team does not on-call for; they may graduate to A after two quarters of stable usage. Each template declares supported runtime versions, maximum drift window before forced upgrade, and the Crossplane claims it may submit.

Adoption instrumentation: Emit events when a template run succeeds or fails (Backstage backend, custom analytics action, or CI webhook). Join those events to catalog entities: services created via template vs forked manually, distribution of shared workflow major versions, and repos that removed required jobs. Build a simple quarterly scorecard for leadership—on-road workload percentage, template success rate (target above 95%), p50 time from scaffold to green production pipeline, tickets per engineer tagged “undifferentiated infra”, and off-road count by reason code (niche runtime, perf, regulator carve-out, legacy freeze). When off-road spikes for “template missing feature”, prioritise roadmap; when spikes for “policy bypass”, tighten admission or escape-gate friction.

Pair quantitative metrics with qualitative DX samples: five developer interviews per quarter focused on last template use. Capture blockers that telemetry cannot see—unclear parameter names, missing multi-region option, fear of lock-in. Publish a “road delta” note with each platform release explaining what changed on the road and which template versions teams should adopt.

Paved roads encode the same template bundle described in golden paths—CI, observability, security defaults—at ecosystem scale: supported runtimes, admission rules, and Crossplane claim types. **Opt-out** is a governed exception with catalog tags and review dates, not silent shadow repos. When template versions advance, run Copier/cruft refresh campaigns or raise off-road counts as a signal that the road is missing capability.

Common failure modes

  • Road on paper only: Slide deck defines the road but clusters still allow unrestricted kubectl—policy and portal tell different stories; align GitOps, RBAC, and catalog tags.
  • Abandoned off-road list: Exceptions proliferate with no expiry; catalog shows hundreds of “temporary” services from years ago; require annual re-approval.
  • Platform as gatekeeper: Every deviation needs a ticket; teams perceive the road as bureaucracy; invest in self-service for road-aligned requests instead.
  • Composition sprawl: Dozens of nearly identical Crossplane compositions; consolidate into parameterized compositions with environment patches.
  • Golden path ≠ road: Teams complete a template but deploy outside GitOps; enforce deploy only via Argo CD Application synced from the scaffolded repo.
  • Metric vanity: Counting template clicks while ignoring production labels inflates adoption; require catalog road: standard and live deploy health before claiming wins.
  • Template zoo: Dozens of unmaintained paved-road scaffolds confuse the portal; retire duplicates and mark a single “official” entry per workload class.
  • Silent off-road deploys: Engineers bypass templates but still use platform clusters; admission and cost allocation must surface unregistered services.

Decision checklist

  • Is the paved road documented with explicit supported/unsupported boundaries and an owner?
  • Can a developer tell within one portal screen whether their service is on-road and what that implies for support?
  • Are Crossplane claims and Kubernetes policies the enforcement mechanism—not slide decks?
  • Is there a time-bound, audited off-road process rather than informal workarounds?
  • Do road upgrades (cluster version, base image, CI template major) have communicated deprecation windows?
  • Are adoption and exception metrics reviewed with engineering leadership each quarter?
  • Does every official paved-road template list owner, semver, supported stacks, and last successful dry-run date?
  • Can platform analytics distinguish template scaffold failures from application CI failures?
  • Are Tier A templates the default in the portal while Tier C requires explicit acknowledgement?

Example use cases

  • Case study — Netflix: Documented “paved road” culture ties supported tooling to full-cycle developer ownership; teams may leave the road but accept operational responsibility—widely cited as a model for voluntary standardisation.
  • Illustrative scenario — payments platform: A card-processing domain standardises on-road services in three PCI zones; off-road batch analytics runs in a segregated account with extra quarterly audits; catalog scorecards show compliance tier per component.
  • Illustrative scenario — multi-team SaaS: After defining the road, Crossplane Environment claims provision namespace + RDS + S3; 85% of new microservices register as on-road within two quarters; platform team reduces “how do I deploy?” tickets by half.
  • Illustrative scenario — template-driven adoption: A retailer ships Tier A Java and Node paved-road templates; portal analytics show 70% of new repos on road within one quarter; FinOps attributes 12% lower orphaned cloud spend as namespaces inherit standard tagging from compositions.
  • Illustrative scenario — scorecard governance: Quarterly reviews show rising off-road “missing GPU profile” exceptions; platform adds a Tier B ML batch template and exception count falls below a fixed threshold without new mandates.
