Data Governance

Last updated: January 2025

What it is

Data governance is the framework of policies, roles, and tooling that ensures data across an organization is defined, owned, protected, and fit for use. It answers who is accountable for a domain, what a field means in business terms, who may access it, how long it may be kept, and how changes propagate through analytics and operational systems.

Operational data governance rests on three pillars. Ownership and stewardship assign business accountability and technical execution for domains such as customer, product, finance, and operations. Cataloging and lineage inventory schemas, pipelines, and datasets while tracing origin, transformation, and downstream consumers—supported by platforms such as DataHub and by cloud-native catalogs in Azure Purview and Google Cloud Dataplex. Policy enforcement ties classification (public, internal, confidential, regulated) to access control, retention, erasure workflows, and quality monitoring so policy is executable, not only documented.

Why it exists

Data crosses every architecture viewpoint in TOGAF—from business definitions to technology storage—yet most organizations still answer “what does this field mean?” through tribal knowledge. When finance, product, and operations each compute “active customer” differently, executive decisions lose a shared factual basis. A governed business glossary, domain ownership, and authoritative metrics turn definitions into managed assets instead of meeting debates.

Privacy and security regimes now expect demonstrable control over information, not good intentions. The NIST Privacy Framework’s identify–govern–control loop assumes you can inventory sensitive data, enforce purpose-bound access, and prove deletion or minimization on request. Cloud-native catalogs (Microsoft Purview, Google Cloud Dataplex, AWS data classification in the Well-Architected Security Pillar) exist precisely because spreadsheets cannot satisfy SAR, erasure, or breach notification timelines at scale.

Analytics and machine learning amplify any governance gap: undocumented lineage, silent schema drift, and unclassified copies in lakes or notebooks bake bias and compliance risk into models that look quantitative. Data governance supplies the metadata, quality signals, and policy enforcement that make downstream AI and reporting auditable extensions of the same control plane—not shadow pipelines.

When to use

  • Personal, health, financial, or payment data triggers statutory or contractual obligations that require classification, retention, and subject-rights workflows.
  • Multiple domains consume the same entities and metrics and need one governed definition layer before building more dashboards or features.
  • Federated or mesh-oriented organizations assign domain teams data products that must publish contracts, quality SLAs, and ownership in a catalog.
  • ML, experimentation, or customer-360 programs need provenance, consent boundaries, and reproducible training datasets.
  • Hybrid or multi-cloud estates where data lands in object stores, warehouses, and SaaS systems without a single natural schema owner.

When NOT to use

  • Pre-product teams with ephemeral datasets and a single analyst—basic naming conventions and backups may be enough until revenue or regulation appears.
  • Programs driven only by audit paperwork without business stewards willing to own definitions and approve access—those produce shelfware, not quality.
  • Attempts to catalog and classify the entire estate before prioritizing high-risk domains; big-bang metadata projects stall before showing value.
  • Replacing broken operational data quality with governance tooling alone, when source systems still lack validation and ownership at ingestion.

Typical architecture

Sources feed metadata and lineage into a catalog; classification and policy enforcement sit between catalog consumers and quality monitoring.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Shared glossary and domain ownership align executives and engineers on one definition of core metrics—reducing rework in BI and product analytics.
  • Classification tags drive least-privilege access, encryption, and masking consistently across warehouses, lakes, and operational stores.
  • Automated lineage from orchestrated pipelines enables impact analysis before schema changes and maps erasure jobs across copies.
  • Integrated quality monitoring (dbt tests, Great Expectations, catalog-published scores) surfaces drift before it reaches dashboards or models.
  • Unified cloud governance planes (Purview, Dataplex, Macie-style discovery) give security and privacy teams continuous visibility instead of annual samples.

Cons

  • Stewardship, legal partnership, and catalog curation require sustained funding—benefits accrue after critical domains are covered.
  • Metadata rots without automated harvest and ownership SLAs; stale catalogs erode trust faster than having no catalog.
  • Top-down policy without domain accountability yields documents that operations never enforce.
  • Retroactive classification of legacy estates is labor-intensive and competes with feature delivery unless phased by risk.
  • Tooling sprawl—separate catalog, quality, and access products—needs integration design or teams work around the stack.

Implementation notes

Start with the data that matters most. Trying to govern all data simultaneously is an approach that fails under its own weight. Identify the organization's most critical data domains — typically the data that drives executive decisions, generates regulatory obligations, or underpins the most-used analytics — and implement governance for those domains first. Establish ownership, write business definitions, classify sensitivity, and document lineage for these domains before expanding to others.

Success in the high-value domains builds credibility and demonstrates the governance program's value.

Automate metadata collection wherever possible. Manual metadata entry is expensive, error-prone, and rarely maintained. Modern data platforms can emit schema changes, lineage events, and data profiling statistics automatically — wire these into the data catalog so that technical metadata stays current without human intervention. Use data quality tools like dbt tests or Great Expectations to continuously measure quality dimensions (completeness, validity, freshness, uniqueness) and publish quality scores to the catalog, creating a live data quality dashboard per domain.

Implement right-to-erasure workflows as a first-class engineering concern, not an afterthought. When an erasure request arrives, the system must locate subject records across operational stores, warehouses, lakes, analytics marts, models, and backups within scope, then delete or anonymize them within applicable regulatory timelines. Lineage in the catalog supplies the map; orchestrated erasure jobs execute propagation. Design identifiers and partition keys so erasure does not require manual forensic search across opaque copies.

Assign accountable owners and deputies per domain; stewards approve glossary changes and access patterns. Emit OpenLineage events from orchestrated jobs so impact analysis and erasure paths stay current. Great Expectations or dbt tests score completeness, uniqueness, freshness, and validity—publish results beside datasets in DataHub or OpenMetadata. Data contracts between producers and consumers state schema, SLAs, and quality thresholds; data mesh extends computational governance so each domain enforces policy locally with shared platforms. Retention schedules tie to classification; legal hold must pause erasure without orphaning contradictory copies in analytics tiers.

Block promotion of new datasets when ownership tags or classification are missing in the catalog—governance gates belong in the same pipeline as schema deploys.

Federated mesh governance needs shared vocabulary for tags and policies—domains differ, but auditors expect one classification enum in exports.

Common failure modes

  • Orphaned stewardship: Owners rotate without succession, so glossary updates, access reviews, and quality SLAs stall. Require deputy stewards, HR-fed ownership alerts, and block new production datasets without an active owner in the catalog.
  • Classification drift: Fields gain identifiers or free-text PII after launch while tags stay “internal.” Run scheduled discovery scans (Macie, Purview, Dataplex classifiers) and tie schema-change tickets to reclassification tasks.
  • Lineage blind spots: Ad hoc scripts, notebook exports, and one-off SaaS syncs never emit metadata, breaking erasure maps and impact analysis. Mandate orchestrated, lineage-aware tooling or a governance gate that rejects undeclared pipelines.
  • Policy without execution: Retention and minimization live in PDFs while storage grows unbounded; failed purge jobs go unmonitored. Treat missed deletion windows as compliance incidents with the same escalation as availability breaches.
  • Catalog as museum: Teams publish metadata once at launch; descriptions, row counts, and quality scores age out. Automate harvest from sources and pipelines, and measure catalog freshness per domain.
  • Governance bypass via copies: Analysts replicate production extracts to unsecured shares or laptops. Pair catalog policy with DLP, signed export paths, and access reviews aligned to NIST-style identify and protect outcomes.

Decision checklist

  • Does every critical domain have a business owner, technical steward, and deputy recorded in the catalog?
  • Is the business glossary authoritative for executive metrics, linked to physical datasets and transformation logic?
  • Are sensitivity labels applied at creation and re-evaluated on schema change, with access enforced via IAM, ABAC, or lake policies?
  • Does lineage cover operational stores, streams, warehouses, lakes, and key SaaS extracts well enough for impact analysis and erasure?
  • Are retention and legal-hold rules automated with tamper-evident deletion or anonymization logs?
  • Can subject access, portability, and erasure requests complete within regulatory clocks using catalog-driven job templates?
  • Do quality tests run continuously with failures routed to domain owners before downstream consumers see bad data?
  • Is the program aligned with compliance architecture controls and enterprise risk tiering (for example NIST Privacy Framework profiles)?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A health-tech platform classifies PHI at ingestion, routes access through role and purpose policies, and blocks ad hoc exports that lack steward approval.
  • Case study — LinkedIn open-sourced DataHub as a metadata graph for discovery, lineage, and ownership across large analytical estates.
  • Case study — Airbnb described its Minerva-based metric layer and tooling so teams share canonical business definitions instead of duplicating SQL semantics.
  • Case study — Microsoft documents Purview as a unified data governance plane spanning catalog, classification, and policy across Azure services.
