Governance & Standards

Tech Radar

A periodic, searchable map of which technologies to adopt, trial, assess, or hold—so teams align without re-running the same evaluations.

⏱ 15 min read

What it is

A tech radar is a visual governance artifact that documents an organization’s current perspective on the technologies, tools, platforms, and techniques it uses or is evaluating. Popularized by Thoughtworks, who publish a public edition twice yearly, the radar organizes items along two axes: quadrants that categorize what kind of thing each entry is, and rings that express the recommended action. Together they give a scannable map of the technology landscape and directional intent.

The four standard quadrants are widely understood: Techniques (practices and patterns such as trunk-based development, fitness functions, or blue-green deployment); Tools (software used in build and operations—Spectral, Trivy, Backstage, and similar); Platforms (managed services, cloud offerings, and shared infrastructure such as Kubernetes, managed Kafka, or vector databases); and Languages & Frameworks (runtimes and application stacks—TypeScript, Python, FastAPI, React). Domain-specific labels are possible, but keeping the classic four preserves legibility for new hires and partners.

The four rings express stance: Adopt means default for new production work when the problem matches; Trial means deliberate production use in bounded contexts with oversight; Assess means watch and experiment locally without wide rollout; Hold means do not start new work on the item—because a better alternative exists, the vendor is sunsetting it, or risk outweighs benefit. Each entry should carry brief rationale, preferred substitutes on Hold, and migration notes where retirement is underway. The radar is not a ban list; it externalizes architecture-community judgment so engineers who missed the meeting can still act consistently.

Radars work best as structured data (YAML or JSON) rendered into a searchable portal edition, refreshed on a committed quarterly or biannual cadence so ring movements record organizational learning over time.

Why it exists

Engineering organizations face a continuous stream of technology choices—frameworks, vendors, cloud services, and techniques. Without a shared evaluation and communication mechanism, three problems compound. Teams duplicate effort proving the same tool; there is no authoritative signal for safe defaults versus experiments or retirement; and proliferation grows because nothing creates friction against yet another stack or creates a path to sunset.

The radar addresses those gaps by publishing architecture-community judgment. When someone asks whether to use a given library on a new service, the radar answers with ring placement plus reasoning—reducing decision latency, unloading senior architects from repeating oral FAQs, and building a shared mental model of direction.

Rings also communicate trajectory, not only snapshot. Movement from Assess to Trial signals rising confidence; Adopt to Hold signals deprecation teams should plan around before CI gates force the issue. Across editions the radar becomes a historical record—which experiments landed, which vendors disappointed, which options matured enough to recommend.

Platform teams often mirror radar Adopt choices in templates and catalog entries so the easiest path to production matches governance intent. That pairing is what turns the radar from a poster on the wall into a default engineers actually experience when they create a service.

When to use

  • Multiple squads choosing stacks independently and accumulating overlapping tooling, support burden, and security patches.
  • Platform or architecture teams that need publishing reach beyond synchronous meetings and hallway decisions.
  • Large transitions—cloud migration, microservices adoption, ML platform build-out—where clear directional signals accelerate alignment.
  • Organizations cultivating deliberate technology adoption, treating choices as governance decisions rather than personal preference.

When not to use

  • Organizations under roughly thirty engineers where a maintained approved-technology list plus architecture office hours covers coordination without a formal edition cycle.
  • Without commitment to a regular review cycle—a radar published once and left to rot misleads more than it helps; do not launch until leadership agrees to quarterly or biannual updates.
  • As a substitute for technology standards enforcement when no scaffolds or policy gates exist—communication alone does not prevent Hold items from spreading.
  • When the architecture community lacks bandwidth to run triage and scoring—an abandoned radar erodes trust faster than informal guidance.

Typical architecture

Operationally, each edition mirrors a quarterly cycle: open nominations, guild triage, a scoring session for ring placement, publication with changelog, and communication—portal update, engineering newsletter, or all-hands segment. The visual grid groups sample entries by quadrant and ring (Techniques versus Tools versus Platforms versus Languages) so readers see density of Adopt defaults and concentration of Hold debt at a glance. Nominations flow through triage and scoring into a versioned dataset, then publish to portal and optional CI policy hooks.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Gives every engineer an immediate, searchable answer to “what should we use for X?” without waiting on architect office hours.
  • Makes technology direction explicit rather than trapped in meeting notes and senior engineers’ heads.
  • Ring movement over time surfaces deprecations and successful trials before they become emergencies.
  • Creates constructive friction against adopting unknown items and signals retirement when paired with scaffolds or dependency policy.

Cons

  • A stale edition misleads worse than no radar—cadence commitment must be honored for trust.
  • Ring placement can turn political when teams lobby for Adopt and resist Hold without objective criteria.
  • Four rings cannot encode “Adopt here, Hold there”; entries need two or three sentences of context to disambiguate.
  • Custom visualization and portal integration cost engineering time; open-source builders reduce that tax when data stays in git.

Implementation notes

Run an open nomination process so any engineer can propose additions, ring moves, or removals. Distribute maintenance beyond the central architecture team and surface “shadow” technologies already in production but absent from the catalog. Use a GitHub issue template, Backstage plugin form, or short web form, and commit to a published response time. The architecture guild triages nominations on a fixed rhythm—weekly or biweekly—deciding whether to resolve as a minor text tweak or escalate to the full scoring session.

The scoring session is where ring placements are decided. Convene senior engineers and architects from major domains; for each candidate item review the problem it solves, production evidence, cost and operability, security posture, and credible alternatives. Reach consensus on ring placement, then capture rationale inside the entry—not only the ring label. A single sentence such as “Hold because vendor EOL is Q4 2027; migrate new services to the Adopt message client before then” earns more trust than an unexplained red dot. Hold entries should always name the preferred substitute and, when known, a sunset or migration window.

Publish each edition as searchable HTML in the developer portal. Generate the interactive visualization from YAML or JSON in git so data can be queried—reports of Hold technologies per service, or dependency diff checks in pull requests. The AOE Technology Radar and Thoughtworks build-your-own-radar tooling accept CSV or JSON and avoid bespoke front-end work. Wire optional CI/CD dependency gates: when lockfiles or manifest files introduce a Hold-listed coordinate, fail the build or require an approved exception from exception management. Align Adopt rings with standards management so golden paths and scaffolds match what the radar endorses.

Close the loop each quarter with a changelog—items added, rings moved, and why—distributed through Slack, email, or an engineering forum. Treat the cadence as a calendar commitment with named owners before shipping edition one; skipping cycles is the fastest path to irrelevance.

During triage, score nominations on production evidence, operational fit, security review status, and duplicate overlap with existing Adopt items. Items lacking a clear problem statement or owner should return to the submitter rather than clogging the scoring agenda. Reserve scoring time for movements that change engineering defaults, vendor sunsets, or enterprise risk—not cosmetic renames.

Thoughtworks quadrants (techniques, tools, platforms, languages) and rings (hold, assess, trial, adopt) are a vocabulary—build your own radar (BYOR) from YAML in git with a quarterly refresh calendar. Nomination intake needs problem statement, sponsor, and production evidence bar; blip moves link to ADRs when they change engineering policy. Publish changelog with each edition; stale radars without dates lose trust faster than empty adopt rings.

Limit hold moves without a named successor—hold entries that only say “avoid” force teams to invent replacements under audit pressure.

Link radar refresh meetings to standards RFC deadlines so adopt ring changes and paved-road updates ship in the same release train.

BYOR tooling should render quadrants and rings from git data, not a quarterly screenshot PDF.

Common failure modes

  • Publish once, never update: The most common failure is shipping a first edition without a non-negotiable quarterly or biannual review. Within six to twelve months entries drift from production reality; teams stop consulting the radar and it becomes an embarrassing artifact. Commit to owners and calendar slots before publication.
  • No Hold enforcement: Hold communicates intent but new services keep adopting deprecated stacks when nothing creates friction. Integrate the Hold list into CI/CD dependency gates—warn, or block, pull requests that add Hold-status libraries unless an exception is recorded and linked in the register.
  • Entries without context: Rings alone cannot distinguish “Hold because legacy” from “Hold because we standardized on something better.” Every entry needs at least two or three sentences: why the ring, who it applies to, and what to use instead on Hold.
  • Quadrant creep: Extra quadrants or custom rings may feel precise but undermine scanability. Keep the four Thoughtworks quadrants and four rings; express nuance in prose (“Trial for batch pipelines; Hold for customer-facing APIs”).

Decision checklist

  • Is there a committed quarterly or biannual cadence with named owners responsible for running the full cycle?
  • Can any engineer nominate items through an open process with published outcomes?
  • Does each entry document ring rationale—not only the ring label—and alternatives for Hold items?
  • Is the radar published in a searchable, portal-integrated format every engineer can reach without VPN quirks?
  • Do CI/CD or scaffolding mechanisms enforce Hold decisions, not merely communicate them?
  • Do Hold entries include migration guidance, timelines, and links to standards or templates?
  • Does each edition ship with a changelog teams can subscribe to?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — Lumen Freight: An engineer starting a new microservice checks the radar before choosing a web framework, sees a first preference on Hold with a documented Adopt alternative, and scaffolds the approved stack in minutes instead of waiting a week for architect back-and-forth.
  • Illustrative scenario — Crestline Health: A quarterly guild review finds three ORMs in production; one moves to Adopt, two to Hold with a twelve-month migration guide; dependency gates flag new violations on the next sprint, and squads on legacy ORMs receive migration playbooks.
  • Case study — Thoughtworks public radar: The consultancy’s biannual edition demonstrates how external publication reinforces engineering culture, signals depth to clients, and gives recruits a tangible view of technology philosophy.
  • Case study — Open-source radar builders: Teams using AOE or Thoughtworks generators keep radar data in git, automate portal deploys, and feed Hold lists into software composition analysis so governance and delivery share one source of truth.
