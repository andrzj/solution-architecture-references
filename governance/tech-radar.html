Governance & Standards

Tech Radar

A periodic, searchable map of which technologies to adopt, trial, assess, or hold—so teams align without re-running the same evaluations.

⏱ 12 min read

What it is

A tech radar is a governance artifact that communicates the organization’s recommended stance on technologies, tools, platforms, and techniques. Popularized by Thoughtworks, it uses four quadrants (Techniques, Tools, Platforms, Languages & Frameworks) and four rings (Adopt, Trial, Assess, Hold) to show what is endorsed for production defaults, what is in limited production use, what deserves attention, and what should not start on new work.

Each entry includes brief rationale—why the item sits in its ring, what to use instead when on Hold, and migration notes where relevant. The radar is not a ban list; it is a shared briefing that reduces duplicated evaluation and makes direction legible to engineers who were not in the architecture meeting where the decision happened.

Radars work best when stored as structured data (YAML or JSON), published to the developer portal, and refreshed on a committed cadence so ring movements tell a story of organizational learning over time.

Why it exists

Engineering organizations constantly evaluate new frameworks, vendors, and practices. Without a published stance, teams repeat the same proofs of concept, senior engineers answer the same “can we use X?” questions, and deprecated tools linger because retirement was never communicated.

The radar externalizes architecture community judgment: an engineer can see that a message broker is Trial in one context but Hold for greenfield services, with a sentence explaining the sunset timeline. That saves calendar time and prevents silent divergence.

Movement between rings signals direction. Shift from Assess to Trial shows growing confidence; Adopt to Hold flags deprecation before dependency gates force migration. Over several editions the radar becomes a readable history of what worked and what did not.

When to use

  • Multiple squads choosing stacks independently and accumulating overlapping tooling.
  • Platform or architecture teams needing an asynchronous channel for technology guidance.
  • Large transitions—cloud migration, observability standardization, service decomposition—where alignment accelerates delivery.
  • Organizations that want deliberate adoption culture instead of ad hoc tool selection per project.

When not to use

  • Very small teams where conversation plus a short approved-technology list is enough overhead.
  • Without commitment to a regular review cycle—a radar published once and left to rot misleads more than it helps.
  • As a substitute for technology standards enforcement when no scaffolds or policy gates exist—communication alone does not prevent Hold items from spreading.

Typical architecture

Nominations flow through triage and scoring into a versioned dataset, then publish to portal and optional CI policy hooks.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Fast answers to technology questions without synchronous architecture review.
  • Captures institutional memory beyond oral tradition and slide decks.
  • Ring movement makes deprecation and experimentation visible early.
  • Reduces proliferation when paired with dependency or scaffold policies.

Cons

  • Stale editions destroy trust; cadence must be non-negotiable.
  • Ring debates can become political without clear criteria.
  • Four rings cannot express every nuance—entries need prose context.
  • Custom radar UIs cost effort; prefer open-source generators when possible.

Implementation notes

Accept nominations from any engineer via a lightweight form or repository template. Guild triage filters duplicates and escalates substantive changes to a scoring session with representatives from major domains.

During scoring, document rationale in the entry itself—Hold items should name the preferred alternative and target sunset where known. Avoid custom quadrants; use prose for edge cases (“Adopt for batch; Hold for online APIs”).

Store radar data in git, generate the interactive view with tools such as the AOE or Thoughtworks build-your-own-radar projects, and embed search in the developer portal. Optional: fail or warn CI when new dependencies match Hold entries. Align rings with standards management lifecycle so Adopt items become golden-path defaults.

Common failure modes

  • One edition wonder: No quarterly or biannual refresh; teams stop consulting the radar.
  • Hold without teeth: Deprecated technologies spread because nothing enforces the signal.
  • Bare rings: Entries list only “Trial” with no explanation—engineers cannot act on the guidance.
  • Quadrant sprawl: Extra categories confuse readers; keep the classic four.

Decision checklist

  • Is there a named owner and fixed review cadence for each radar edition?
  • Can any engineer nominate items, with transparent outcomes?
  • Does every entry include rationale and alternatives for Hold items?
  • Is the radar searchable from the developer portal?
  • Are Hold technologies optionally gated in CI or scaffolding?
  • Do ring changes ship with a changelog teams can subscribe to?

Example use cases

  • New microservice bootstrap: A team finds their preferred web framework on Hold with a link to the Adopt default; they scaffold the approved stack in minutes.
  • Illustrative scenario — ORM consolidation: Radar review moves one object-relational mapper to Adopt and two legacy options to Hold with a twelve-month migration guide; dependency gates flag new violations immediately.
  • Case study — Thoughtworks public radar: The consultancy’s biannual edition demonstrates how external publication reinforces engineering culture and signals depth to clients and recruits.
