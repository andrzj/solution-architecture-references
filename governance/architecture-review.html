Architecture Review Process

Structured forum to evaluate significant design changes before they commit the organization to costly paths.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

Architecture review is a structured process in which stakeholders evaluate a proposed or existing system design against agreed concerns, principles, and constraints before commitment or before risky change ships. ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 frames the underlying artifacts: architecture descriptions express how views address stakeholder concerns, and review is how those descriptions get challenged while options and consequences remain visible.

Most organizations implement review as a workflow: an author publishes a proposal (RFC, design doc, or ADR), reviewers triage scope and risk, a forum discusses trade-offs, and the outcome is a recorded decision with follow-up actions. TOGAF’s architecture governance model expects comparable checkpoints so solutions conform to enterprise direction. The goal is not ceremonial sign-off but informed consent among the teams who will live with the design.

Why it exists

Significant design choices are expensive to reverse. Data store selection, public API shape, tenancy model, and cross-region failover strategy bind teams for years. Review exists to surface integration conflicts, security gaps, operability blind spots, and cost drivers while change is still cheap—consistent with risk-informed practices in the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and with well-architected review guidance from major cloud providers.

When review works, it also spreads judgment: reviewers learn from each other’s failures and constraints, which lifts baseline design skill across the organization. Martin Fowler’s conversational scaling idea and Thoughtworks’ emphasis on recorded decisions both treat review as teaching at speed, not as a static approval stamp.

When to use

  • New platforms or capabilities that other teams will depend on or operate.
  • Material changes to shared data stores, identity flows, or customer-facing APIs.
  • Regulated, safety-critical, or high-availability systems where design evidence supports audit and incident readiness.
  • Initiatives with material spend, long delivery horizons, or irreversible vendor or cloud commitments.

When not to use

  • Routine feature work inside an established bounded context with patterns already encoded in platform defaults and ADRs.
  • Spikes and time-boxed experiments whose explicit purpose is to learn before a durable architecture is chosen.
  • Decisions already covered by an adopted golden path where review would only duplicate automated guardrails.

Typical architecture

Proposals enter triage, meet a review forum matched to risk, and exit as a decision record plus tracked actions and exceptions.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Surfaces cross-team dependencies and failure modes before build contracts harden.
  • Produces durable decision records that support onboarding, audits, and later supersession.
  • Distributes architectural expertise by exposing reviewers to diverse problem domains.
  • Aligns delivery with enterprise principles and cloud well-architected lenses when checklists are explicit.

Cons

  • Poorly scoped review mandates add calendar latency without proportional risk reduction.
  • Reviews without enforcement of outcomes become rubber stamps that teams learn to bypass.
  • Large forums can dilute accountability when no single owner tracks conditions and actions.
  • Over-formal templates discourage honest exploration of alternatives.

Implementation notes

Define objective triggers—customer data classes, blast radius, regulatory scope, net-new external dependencies—so teams self-identify when review is required. Publish a concise RFC or ADR template aligned with Nygard’s context, decision, and consequences structure and store records in version control beside the affected systems.

Time-box review meetings and default to “approve with conditions” rather than open-ended debate. Assign a decision owner who converts discussion into action items with owners and due dates. For cloud workloads, map review checklists to AWS Well-Architected, Azure Well-Architected, or Google Cloud architecture framework pillars so reviewers use a shared vocabulary.

Measure whether conditions are closed, whether superseded ADRs are linked, and whether exceptions expire—not how many meetings were held. Integrate outcomes with exception management when teams must deviate temporarily from standards.

Common failure modes

  • Late-stage theater: Review happens after implementation commitments, so feedback only produces debt tickets. Move intake to design or RFC phase.
  • Missing follow-through: Conditions live only in meeting notes. Track them in the same system as engineering work.
  • Wrong forum: Every proposal goes to the largest board. Route local decisions to guild or async review.
  • Unreadable packets: Proposals lack views for security, operations, or data concerns. Require explicit sections mapped to stakeholder concerns per ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010.

Decision checklist

  • Are review triggers published and based on objective risk signals rather than politics?
  • Does each proposal list options, rejected alternatives, and measurable consequences?
  • Are outcomes recorded as ADRs or equivalent and discoverable from the developer portal?
  • Are conditions and exceptions tracked with owners, expiry, and linkage to compliance scope?
  • Does the review checklist cover security, reliability, cost, and operability explicitly?
  • Is there a path to supersede or revisit decisions when context changes?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A payments team proposes a new event backbone for settlement notifications; review focuses on ordering guarantees, key management, and PCI-scoped data flows before any cluster is provisioned.
  • Case study — Amazon documents Well-Architected reviews as a structured conversation across operational excellence, security, reliability, performance, cost, and sustainability before major workload changes.
  • Case study — GitHub’s public ADR repositories show how organizations keep consequential infrastructure choices reviewable and versioned alongside code.
