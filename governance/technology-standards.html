Governance & Standards

Technology Standards

Curated lists of languages, runtimes, and patterns teams should use—plus how exceptions work.

⏱ 16 min read

What it is

Technology standards are the organization’s curated defaults for languages, runtimes, platforms, and integration patterns that teams should use when building and operating software. A standard is more than a PDF on a wiki: it names what is approved for production, what is allowed only with an exception, and what is retired—with rationale, owners, and review dates so engineers can decide without opening a ticket for every choice.

Effective standards programs pair human-readable catalogs with mechanical enforcement. Golden-path templates and Internal Developer Platform (IDP) scaffolds instantiate approved stacks; policy-as-code in CI/CD and infrastructure pipelines rejects non-compliant artifacts before they reach production. An exceptions registry links time-boxed waivers to specific services and compensating controls, so deviation stays visible rather than becoming shadow IT.

Standards complement—but do not replace—the tech radar and architecture review. The radar expresses directional preference (adopt, trial, assess, hold); standards convert stable “adopt” decisions into defaults that new services inherit automatically.

Why it exists

Unconstrained technology choice compounds operational cost faster than feature velocity. Every additional database engine, service mesh, log format, and authentication pattern requires someone to patch CVEs, train on-call engineers, and debug incidents at 3 a.m. Standards encode lessons from past outages and migrations into defaults so teams benefit from organizational learning without repeating expensive experiments.

Security and compliance baselines depend on predictability. Auditors and customers ask which encryption libraries, identity providers, and data stores are in scope; a standards catalog answers that question consistently. Without standards, each team interprets “secure by default” differently, and gaps appear only after an assessment or breach.

Standards also reduce cognitive load for engineers joining new teams or spinning up greenfield services. When the approved observability stack, container base image, and API gateway are one scaffold command away, teams spend time on product problems instead of re-litigating infrastructure choices.

When to use

  • Platform engineering or enterprise architecture programs that need shared defaults across many squads.
  • Organizations establishing security baselines, SOC 2 scope, or industry regulatory guardrails.
  • Post-merger integration where acquired companies bring overlapping stacks that must converge.
  • Outsourced or nearshore development where contracts require explicit approved technology lists.
  • High-churn engineering organizations where onboarding speed depends on consistent golden paths.

When not to use

  • Research labs or innovation charters explicitly funded to explore unproven technologies outside production constraints.
  • Very early startups where the entire engineering staff fits in one room and informal agreement suffices—formal standards can wait until coordination pain appears.
  • When leadership will not fund enforcement (templates, linters, policy gates); unpublished standards that nobody follows create cynicism without reducing risk.

Typical architecture

Standards flow from documented decisions into scaffolding, automated checks, and exception tracking tied to the service catalog.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Faster onboarding and fewer “which database?” debates for every new service.
  • Lower security and operations cost through shared patching, monitoring, and runbook coverage.
  • Audit-ready answers to what runs in production and which versions are supported.
  • Clear sunset path for deprecated technologies via hold/retire states and scaffold restrictions.

Cons

  • Perceived rigidity if exception paths are slow or opaque—teams bypass standards instead of using them.
  • Risk of stale standards that block better options when review cadence slips.
  • Upfront investment in policy-as-code and portal integration before benefits are visible.
  • Central teams can become bottlenecks if every deviation requires synchronous approval.

Implementation notes

Co-create standards with product and platform engineers, not as architecture mandates handed down after decisions are made. Publish RFCs or ADRs for each standard change, and tie major updates to quarterly architecture forums. Keep the approved list small enough to operate: a dozen well-supported defaults beat fifty options nobody maintains.

Encode standards in the IDP first—scaffold commands, starter repos, and pipeline templates should default to approved stacks so compliance is the path of least resistance. Add policy-as-code second: OPA/Rego or cloud-specific guardrails for container images, Terraform modules, and dependency versions. Fail builds on hold/retire technologies rather than relying on wiki reminders.

Measure adoption through scaffold usage, policy pass rates, and percentage of new services on current standard versions—not through attestation surveys. Pair standards with exception management so teams can move fast during migrations while debt stays visible and time-boxed.

Standards name the approved runtime, datastore, messaging, and observability stack with supported major versions—not vague “use Postgres.” Paved-road defaults in scaffolds are mandates teams can start from; stricter org policies block hold/retire coordinates at build time. Distinguish “allowed with review” trials from adopt-tier defaults so engineers know when an RFC is required. RFC workflow, radar cadence, and deprecation governance sit in standards management; here document what is on the paved road this quarter.

Publish migration snippets from hold to adopt tiers beside each standard change so teams do not grep Slack for the blessed Dockerfile.

Inner-source fixes to scaffolds should merge through the same RFC path as policy changes so defaults stay auditable.

Mandates without paved roads invite exceptions—every adopt-tier standard needs a working scaffold command.

Common failure modes

  • Shelf standards: Documents exist but scaffolds and CI still allow anything; engineers learn to ignore the catalog.
  • Ever-growing approve list: New tools are added but nothing moves to hold/retire, so operational load only increases.
  • Exception permanence: Waivers renew automatically and become the de facto standard without updating the catalog.
  • Version ambiguity: “PostgreSQL” as a standard without a supported major version leaves teams on unsupported releases.

Decision checklist

  • Do golden-path scaffolds default to approved stacks with pinned versions?
  • Does CI or deployment policy block new usage of hold/retire technologies?
  • Is there a published exception process with expiry, owner, and compensating controls?
  • Are standards linked from PR templates and the developer portal service catalog?
  • Is there a quarterly review cadence with named owners per standard domain?
  • Does the catalog distinguish adopt vs trial vs hold with clear definitions?

Example use cases

  • Cloud security baseline: All new HTTP services must use the approved managed relational database, OIDC via the corporate IdP, and OpenTelemetry export to the shared collector—enforced by scaffold defaults and a failing CI check on non-compliant Helm values.
  • Illustrative scenario — payment platform harmonization: After two product lines merge, architecture publishes a single approved message bus and retires duplicate brokers; migration exceptions are granted for six months with traffic dashboards tracking cutover progress.
  • Case study — Spotify Backstage: Technology standards and templates surfaced through a developer portal reduced duplicate tooling choices by making the recommended path discoverable next to each service’s catalog entry.
