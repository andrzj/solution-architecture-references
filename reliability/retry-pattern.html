Reliability & Resilience Essential

Retry Pattern

Reattempting failed operations when the cause is transient and the operation is safe to repeat.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

The Retry pattern is a client-side resilience mechanism that re-executes a failed operation after a bounded delay when the failure is classified as transient and the operation is idempotent (or otherwise safe to repeat). Azure Architecture Center defines it as an automatic response to failures that are likely to resolve without changing request payload or infrastructure. Polly and Resilience4j express the same idea as a declarative retry policy: maximum attempts, backoff, predicates on exception types, and optional integration with circuit breakers.

In production, a retry is never “try again forever.” Google’s SRE material on handling overload treats aggressive clients as part of the failure mode: each attempt must honor a per-call timeout, respect server signals such as HTTP 429 and Retry-After, and stop before retries convert a brief blip into a load amplifier. AWS’s Builders Library guidance on timeouts and retries pairs bounded retries with jittered backoff so recovering fleets are not hit by synchronized waves.

Retry applies at the call boundary within a single logical operation (HTTP/gRPC stub, SDK wrapper, database driver)—not as a substitute for message-broker redelivery or batch schedulers. Michael Nygard’s Release It! warns that uncontrolled retries are a classic source of cascading failure; retries belong in a deliberate stack with timeouts, backoff, circuit breaking, and bulkheads.

Why it exists

Distributed systems routinely see failures that disappear in seconds: connection resets during rollouts, brief DNS saturation, managed-database failovers, or upstream 503 responses while autoscaling adds capacity. Without retries, those events burn error budget and page humans for problems that self-heal. SRE practice assumes partial failure is normal; callers should absorb transients when doing so is safe and within latency budgets.

Without classification, caps, and backoff, retries do the opposite of help. Every caller hammering a struggling dependency extends the outage—exactly the retry-storm dynamic Nygard and AWS document. A mature retry policy is selective (retry only what can succeed), bounded (attempt limit plus overall deadline), and observable (metrics on retry rate and retry success).

Retries are also a contract between teams: the caller signals tolerance for brief unavailability, and the dependency team publishes which status codes and rate-limit headers are safe to honor. When that contract is explicit in runbooks and SDK defaults, retries reduce pager noise without hiding chronic dependency debt.

When to use

  • Operations are idempotent or protected by idempotency keys (safe GET/PUT, deduplicated writes, Kafka idempotent producer).
  • Errors are transient: timeouts, connection resets, HTTP 429/503/504, throttling, brief pool exhaustion.
  • The caller has latency headroom in its SLO—total time including retries stays within the user-facing budget.
  • SDK or platform defaults exist (AWS/GCP/Azure clients) and you are tuning them to match your SLOs, not disabling safety limits.
  • Retries are composed with circuit breakers and fallbacks so sustained outages fail fast instead of looping.
  • Read paths where a slightly stale second attempt (after a cache miss or routing blip) still satisfies the SLO better than surfacing an error immediately.
  • Platform-managed clients already expose tunable maxAttempts aligned with their backing service’s retry guidance—your job is to align service deadlines with those defaults.

When not to use

  • Operations are not idempotent (charges, one-shot POSTs, irreversible state transitions) without explicit deduplication.
  • Failures are permanent: HTTP 4xx validation/auth errors will not heal with time.
  • The dependency is in sustained outage—open the circuit breaker and route to fallback instead of retrying.
  • Your deadline is tighter than one retry plus backoff—fail fast and surface the error.
  • Message brokers already redeliver—stacking application retries on broker retries without understanding combined semantics.
  • Highly contended resources where each retry competes for the same lock or reservation slot and almost always fails again (prefer queuing or a different strategy).
  • Health-critical or safety-adjacent operations where repeating without human confirmation is unacceptable, even if technically idempotent on the server.
  • Global retry policies copied from another service without measuring that dependency’s error taxonomy—429 on one API may mean quota, on another may mean permanent ban.

Typical architecture

Call-site wrapper classifies errors, sleeps with backoff, and stops at max attempts before returning failure upstream.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Improves perceived availability for transient faults without changing downstream architecture.
  • Available in mainstream libraries (Resilience4j, Polly, AWS SDK, gRPC retry policies).
  • Reduces noise in on-call when paired with proper error taxonomy and SLO budgets.
  • Composable first layer in Azure’s recommended resilience pipeline (retry → breaker → bulkhead).
  • Respects platform hints when Retry-After headers are honored.
  • Moves transient failure handling closer to the call site, where status codes and timeouts are known, instead of masking everything as a generic 500 upstream.

Cons

  • Increases tail latency and multiplies load on failing dependencies when uncapped.
  • Dangerous without idempotency—duplicate side effects are among the costliest production bugs.
  • Masks chronic dependency degradation if retry metrics are not monitored.
  • Retry storms synchronize with fixed or un-jittered backoff schedules.
  • Does not replace fixing root causes or designing for overload (SRE handling-overload guidance).
  • Duplicate attempts can confuse tracing unless spans or logs annotate each try with attempt number and outcome.

Implementation notes

Maintain two explicit exception or status-code sets: retryable and non-retryable. In HTTP, treat 429/503 as retryable only when the operation is idempotent; never retry most 4xx. Configure per-attempt timeouts shorter than the end-to-end deadline (AWS Builders Library: timeouts are not optional decoration—they define worst-case resource holding time).

Cap attempts (often three to five on synchronous paths) and enforce an overall deadline. Use exponential backoff with jitter between attempts; see the dedicated backoff article. Consider a retry budget (limit fraction of outbound traffic that may be retries) to protect shared dependencies during incidents.

For gRPC, map UNAVAILABLE, DEADLINE_EXCEEDED (when the server may not have processed work), and certain ABORTED cases to retryable sets; never retry INVALID_ARGUMENT or PERMISSION_DENIED. Azure’s resiliency guidance recommends documenting these sets per integration in the same repository as client configuration.

Resilience4j Retry and Polly WaitAndRetryAsync support predicates, backoff suppliers, and event callbacks for metrics. Wire retries before circuit breakers in the decoration order so breakers see aggregated failure rates. Export retries_total, retry_success_total, and latency histograms including retry attempts.

Load-test with retries enabled: chaos or fault injection should confirm that peak retry rate stays below the dependency’s published RPS ceiling and that thread pools do not fill when timeouts are misconfigured.

Retry budgets: Cap the fraction of outbound calls that may be retries—Linkerd-style retry budgets prevent a fleet-wide incident from converting every first attempt into three. Implement as a sliding window on retry-labeled metrics or as a dedicated rate limiter in front of the retry wrapper. When the budget exhausts, fail fast and surface degradation instead of amplifying overload on a healing dependency.

Deadline propagation: Propagate a monotonically shrinking deadline (gRPC Context deadline, HTTP grpc-timeout or internal X-Request-Deadline). Each attempt’s timeout must be the minimum of the configured per-try limit and time left on the parent deadline—never spawn attempt four when the caller has already timed out.

Retry amplification: Uncoordinated retries multiply across layers. If mobile, edge gateway, BFF, and service each allow three attempts, worst-case load on a shared dependency scales as 3layers—four layers is 81 invocations per user action. Centralise retry policy at the outermost caller that owns the user deadline, or disable inner retries when an outer wrapper already retries.

Error taxonomy (HTTP): Typically retry 408 Request Timeout, 429, 502, 503, 504, and occasional dependent 500 when the body indicates a transient fault. Do not retry most other 4xx—they encode client or business errors that will not heal with delay. For 429/503, parse Retry-After (seconds or HTTP-date) and sleep at least that long before the next attempt. See Exponential Backoff with Jitter for full, equal, and decorrelated jitter formulas (AWS Architecture Blog).

Idempotency gate: Retry only when the operation is safe to repeat—idempotent HTTP methods, idempotency keys on writes, or deduplicated message IDs. Payment and inventory writes without keys must fail fast, not retry blindly.

Hedged requests: For idempotent read paths with tight latency SLOs, issue a duplicate request after a delay (often near p95) and take whichever completes first—cancel the slower call. Hedging multiplies load; cap concurrent hedges, use only on cacheable reads, and never on writes. Google’s Tail at Scale and gRPC hedging docs describe when the latency win justifies the extra traffic.

Breaker interaction: Place retry inside or ahead of the circuit breaker only while the breaker is closed—retries record failures into the sliding window. When the breaker is open, skip retries and fail fast or fallback immediately so half-open probes are not drowned in retry traffic.

Common failure modes

  • Retrying non-idempotent writes — duplicate payments, emails, or inventory movements.
  • No timeout on each attempt — threads blocked until the global deadline; cascading pool exhaustion per Release It!
  • Retrying permanent errors — 400/401/404 loops waste budget and hide bugs.
  • Ignoring Retry-After — violates upstream throttle contract and deepens overload.
  • No breaker or budget — retries continue through prolonged outages, amplifying failure.
  • Nested retries — mobile client, API gateway, and microservice each retry the same call, multiplying attempts without coordination.
  • Logging only final failure — operators cannot distinguish “failed once” from “failed on attempt five,” slowing incident triage.

Decision checklist

  • Is every retried operation idempotent or covered by an idempotency key?
  • Are per-attempt timeouts and a global deadline configured?
  • Is error classification explicit (status codes, gRPC codes, exception types)?
  • Are max attempts, backoff with jitter, and optional retry budget set per dependency?
  • Does a circuit breaker halt retries during sustained failure?
  • Are retry rate and retry-success metrics dashboards and alerts in place?
  • Does the policy respect Retry-After on 429/503 responses?
  • Have you verified combined retry depth across gateways, mesh sidecars, and application code?
  • Do distributed traces or structured logs include attempt index and retryable classification?

Example use cases

  • Case study — AWS SDK defaults: Service clients ship adaptive retry modes that combine attempt caps with backoff; teams tune mode and max attempts to match service SLOs rather than disabling retries entirely.
  • Illustrative scenario — Nordac Retail checkout: The order service retries a 503 from the PayForge processor using an idempotency key on authorize; after three attempts the circuit opens and the storefront shows “payment paused—cart saved” without double-charging.
  • Illustrative scenario — Fleetline mesh egress: A gRPC client in the dispatch service retries UNAVAILABLE during a rolling restart of the RouteMap cluster, then stops when the breaker records sustained failures from that dependency.

Related patterns

  • Exponential Backoff with Jitter — delay schedule between retry attempts.
  • Circuit Breaker — stops retries when failure is sustained.
  • Bulkhead Pattern — isolates retry/thread pools per dependency.
  • Idempotency — safety property that makes retries permissible.
