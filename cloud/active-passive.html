Active-Passive Architecture

Primary/standby patterns with failover for disaster recovery.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

Active-passive (primary/standby) serves production traffic from one region or site while a secondary site stays warm but idle—or lightly loaded—until failover. The passive site receives replication from the primary so database promotion and DNS cutover can meet RTO without paying for full active-active capacity.

It is the most common enterprise DR pattern: cheaper than active-active while still meeting RTO targets measured in minutes when warm standby and rehearsed runbooks are in place.

Why it exists

Organisations need better RTO than backup-and-restore but cannot justify full duplicate capacity in every region.

Regulators often require a geographically separated recovery site even when daily traffic is concentrated in one region.

When to use

  • Business-critical apps with RTO of 5–60 minutes and RPO of seconds to minutes.
  • Database-heavy systems where Aurora Global Database or cross-region read replica promotion is the primary DR investment.
  • Teams with rehearsed failover runbooks and DNS or Global Accelerator health-checked routing.

When not to use

  • Passive region never tested — failover fails at 3 AM because credentials, secrets, or scaling scripts are stale.
  • Active-passive masquerading as active-active (both regions get writes) without conflict handling.

Typical architecture

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Lower cost than active-active while meeting moderate RTO.
  • Clear operational story: one primary writer simplifies consistency.
  • Failover drills can use passive region without customer traffic.

Cons

  • RTO includes detection, decision, DNS TTL, DB promotion, and scale-out — often underestimated.
  • Replication lag defines real RPO; async replication loses in-flight writes.
  • Passive resources still incur storage and minimal compute cost.

Implementation notes

Automate promotion steps where possible (Aurora global failover, ECS/Kubernetes scale policies triggered by runbook). Keep secrets and container images identical in both regions.

Measure replication lag continuously; alert when lag exceeds RPO budget. Run quarterly GameDays that execute full failover including DNS flip.

Common failure modes

  • Promotion without validation: Standby promoted while replication was broken — data loss exceeds RPO.
  • DNS stickiness: Clients cache old IP addresses; failover appears partial — lower TTL on failover records.

Decision checklist

  • Has full failover been executed end-to-end in the last 90 days?
  • Is passive region infrastructure defined in IaC with the same modules as primary?
  • Are write dependencies (queues, caches) replicated or rebuilt during failover?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A billing API runs active in one region with a global database replica and a passive cluster at quarter scale; rehearsed failover promotes the replica, scales the standby fleet, and flips DNS in under ten minutes.
  • Illustrative scenario — A B2B SaaS control plane keeps a pilot-light passive region (network and data replication only); on regional failure, infrastructure-as-code reprovisions compute and restores service within the agreed RTO.
