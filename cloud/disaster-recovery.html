Cloud Architecture Reliability

Disaster Recovery Patterns

The four cloud DR strategies — from cheap-but-slow backup-restore to expensive-but-instant multi-site active-active — and how to choose between them based on your RTO and RPO requirements.

⏱ 11 min read

What it is

Disaster recovery (DR) is the set of processes, infrastructure, and runbooks that restore applications and data after a major failure—regional cloud outage, datacenter loss, ransomware, or a bad deployment that cannot be rolled back in place. Architects express recovery goals with two metrics: Recovery Time Objective (RTO), the maximum acceptable downtime before service is restored, and Recovery Point Objective (RPO), the maximum acceptable data loss, usually described as how far back in time a consistent restore point must exist.

If leadership sets RPO to one hour and an incident occurs at 14:00, operations must be able to restore the system to at least a consistent state as of 13:00. Tighter RTO and RPO generally require more replicated data, pre-provisioned capacity, and automated failover; looser targets allow cheaper backup-and-restore designs. Business stakeholders own the numbers; engineering owns whether the architecture and testing actually meet them.

Hyperscalers and enterprise frameworks describe the same cost-versus-speed spectrum under different names. The AWS disaster-recovery whitepaper frames four patterns: backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, and multi-site active-active. Azure’s Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery (BCDR) guidance maps similar tiers to paired regions and recovery orchestration. The choice is not “which product” but which tier of standby capacity and automation matches the agreed RTO and RPO for each workload tier.

Why it exists

Cloud regions and availability zones are highly reliable, but they are not immune to large-scale failures, misconfigurations, or dependency outages. A workload deployed in a single region with backups only in that region can remain unavailable until the region recovers or until engineers rebuild elsewhere from snapshots—a timeline that often exceeds what customers or regulators tolerate.

DR exists so recovery is a rehearsed engineering outcome, not improvisation during an incident. The AWS Well-Architected Reliability pillar expects you to define recovery objectives per workload, use multiple locations where the business requires it, and test recovery before you need it. Google’s SRE practice treats failure as inevitable and invests in runbooks, game days, and measurable recovery time rather than assuming uptime from a single site.

Mismatch between stated RTO/RPO and built infrastructure is a common governance gap. Backups in object storage support restore-after-loss but do not by themselves deliver minute-level RTO; that requires warm capacity, health-checked routing, and promotion paths that teams have exercised under realistic conditions.

When to use

  • The organisation has classified workloads by business impact and assigned RTO/RPO targets that leadership has signed off—not informal “we’ll fix it quickly” assumptions.
  • Revenue, safety, or regulatory obligations require geographic separation between production and recovery sites.
  • You need a documented DR strategy per tier (backup/restore through active-active) so platform and application teams provision the right level of replication and standby compute.
  • Audit or certification requires evidence of tested recovery, not only backup retention policies.
  • Multi-region or multi-AZ placement is already on the roadmap for latency or capacity, and DR can align with that topology instead of bolt-on standby later.

When not to use

  • One-size DR for every system: Running full active-active for internal sandboxes wastes budget; tier DR investment to business impact per application.
  • Active-active without data design: Multi-writer active-active for a single-primary relational monolith invites split-brain and conflicting writes; redesign data ownership or choose active-passive failover instead.
  • Pilot light without automation: A minimal standby region that depends on manual DNS edits and ad-hoc scaling at 03:00 will miss RTO even if the architecture diagram looks correct.
  • DR documentation only: Runbooks that have never been executed in a controlled test do not qualify as a DR capability under Well-Architected or most audit frameworks.
  • Backups as the sole control: Backups satisfy retention and legal hold but do not replace failover architecture when RTO is measured in minutes.

Typical architecture

The four DR patterns trade monthly standby cost against how quickly traffic and data can resume after primary site loss. Architects usually map tier-1 workloads to warm standby or active-active, tier-2 to pilot light or warm standby, and non-critical estates to backup and restore.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Public cloud DR avoids capital expenditure on duplicate physical datacenters; you pay for standby capacity and replication as operational cost aligned to tier.
  • Managed database global replication and object-store cross-region replication shorten RPO for many workloads without application rewrites.
  • Infrastructure as code lets you provision matching stacks in a recovery region from the same modules and pipelines as production.
  • Block-level replication services support lift-and-shift estates that were not born cloud-native.
  • Controlled failure injection and game days validate recovery without waiting for a real regional outage.

Cons

  • Untested DR creates false confidence; documentation alone does not reduce downtime when failover is first attempted under incident pressure.
  • Multi-site active-active shifts complexity to data consistency, conflict resolution, and observability across regions.
  • Ongoing cross-region replication and standby compute increase monthly spend and must be governed like production capacity.
  • Achievable RTO includes detection time, human approval, DNS caching, database promotion, and scale-out—each step adds minutes that slide decks often omit.
  • Passive DR environments drift from production unless drift detection and periodic rehearsed failover keep them aligned.

Implementation notes

Start from business-classified tiers: document RTO/RPO per workload in an architecture decision record or service catalog, then map each tier to one of the four DR patterns. Use the same tagging and account boundaries as your landing zone so recovery regions, backup vaults, and networking attachments are discoverable during an incident.

Replicate data and configuration artifacts across region boundaries—database replicas or managed global stores, cross-region backup copies, secrets and parameters, container images, and IaC state. Keep failover records on global DNS or anycast front ends at low TTL during steady state so cutover does not wait on client cache expiry.

Automate the repeatable steps: health-check-driven routing changes, managed database failover or promotion APIs, and scale-out of standby compute. Chain them in a workflow with explicit status output to the incident channel. Reserve manual steps for judgment calls (confirming primary is truly lost, halting split-brain writes) and document those steps so any on-call engineer can execute them.

Test recovery on a cadence proportional to tier: tier-1 workloads should run game days that measure achieved RTO and RPO against targets; lower tiers need at least periodic restore drills. Capture gaps in the runbook and fix drift before the next review cycle.

Map each business tier to backup-and-restore, pilot light, warm standby, or active-active with explicit RTO and RPO numbers in the catalog—not narrative-only DR slides. Maintain a dependency graph: failover order starts with identity and DNS, then data promotion, then stateless scale-out. Immutable or air-gapped backup copies (object lock, vault lock, WORM retention) defend against ransomware that deletes live replicas and online snapshots together. Quarterly restore tests must prove bootstraps, secrets, and runbooks still match production versions; untested backups are inventory, not recovery. Align with multi-region residency rules so recovery regions stay inside legal boundaries.

Time-box restore drills and publish achieved RTO versus target; gaps belong in the platform backlog with the same priority as feature work.

Common failure modes

  • Undocumented DR strategy: Leadership remembers an informal RTO from a past workshop while no runbook or test exists; the first real failover exposes hours of unplanned work.
  • Backups colocated with primary: Snapshots and backup vaults that exist only in the failed region cannot be restored until that region or a copy elsewhere is reachable.
  • Secrets and config missing in DR: Standby compute starts but applications fail because credentials, feature flags, or license keys were never replicated or parameterized for the recovery site.
  • DNS TTL too high: Routing flips at the edge while clients continue hitting the failed endpoint until cached records expire.
  • Stateful tiers not DR-aware: Session, cache, or queue state living in a single region is lost on failover even when the database recovers cleanly.
  • Untested promotion under load: Replication lag spikes during primary stress; failover is invoked without checking lag and RPO is silently violated.

Decision checklist

  • Have business stakeholders formally agreed on RTO and RPO per workload and recorded them where auditors and on-call engineers can find them?
  • Are backups and continuous replication copies stored in a recovery region separate from the primary?
  • Has end-to-end failover or restore been executed recently, with measured RTO and RPO compared to targets?
  • Is the recovery environment defined in the same IaC and deployment pipelines as production, with drift detection?
  • Are secrets, images, and non-database dependencies available in the recovery region before you need them?
  • Can on-call staff execute the documented failover path without tribal knowledge from a single architect?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A regulated payments platform uses warm standby in a paired region: globally replicated relational data, a small always-on compute footprint in the recovery site, and quarterly game days that time promotion plus DNS cutover against a fifteen-minute RTO target.
  • Illustrative scenario — A customer-facing API accepts backup-and-restore for a reporting subdomain but uses pilot light for the order API: replicated data and networking in a second region, with automation to scale application tiers when health checks fail in the primary region.
  • Case study — Netflix and other large streamers have published multi-region active architectures with automated traffic steering when a region degrades; the pattern trades cost for near-continuous availability during regional failure.

Related patterns

  • Active-Passive Architecture — Primary/standby failover is the most common way to implement warm standby and pilot-light DR tiers.
  • Active-Active Architecture — Multi-site active-active is the highest tier on the DR spectrum when RTO near zero justifies duplicate capacity.
  • Cloud Networking — Failover routing, private connectivity, and cross-region networking underpin every DR design.
  • Cloud Landing Zones — Separate accounts or subscriptions for production and recovery simplify blast radius and audit evidence.
  • Reliability & Resilience — DR complements intra-region resilience patterns such as bulkheads and graceful degradation.
