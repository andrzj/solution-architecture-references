Observability & Operations Essential

Three Pillars of Observability

The three pillars—metrics, logs, and traces—are the complementary signals teams correlate with OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Grafana Loki, and trace backends to understand production behavior, including failures nobody scripted in advance.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

The three pillars of observability are the complementary telemetry types—metrics, logs, and traces—that together let engineers infer the internal state of a running system from external outputs. None of the three alone is sufficient at scale; each answers different questions with different cost and fidelity tradeoffs.

  • Metrics are numeric time series aggregated over time—request rate, error ratio, saturation, and latency percentiles. They are cheap to retain in Prometheus or Grafana Mimir and ideal for dashboards, SLO burn alerts, and the Four Golden Signals from the Google SRE book, but they are intentionally lossy because aggregation discards per-request detail.
  • Logs are timestamped, discrete event records, usually structured as JSON lines. They carry the highest per-event fidelity—error codes, business identifiers, and human-oriented messages—and land in systems such as Grafana Loki or Elasticsearch when you need line-level search during incidents.
  • Traces are directed graphs of spans that follow a single request across service boundaries. They show where latency accumulated and how errors propagated, which is essential when a user-visible slowdown spans many hops nobody documented in a runbook.

OpenTelemetry (OTel) is the vendor-neutral CNCF standard that produces all three signal types with one SDK and routes them through a Collector to backends such as Prometheus for metrics, Grafana Tempo or Jaeger for traces, and Loki for logs. The related MELT model adds Events (deploys, feature-flag flips, scaling actions) as temporal anchors when correlating charts across Grafana.

Observability is not the same as monitoring. Monitoring answers whether a known symptom crossed a threshold; observability supports exploratory questions about novel failures Charity Majors describes—behaviors you did not pre-chart. Honeycomb’s event-centric products popularized that mindset; the three pillars are the portable version most teams implement with Prometheus, Loki, and traces in Grafana.

Why it exists

Distributed architectures shattered the assumption that failures arrive with obvious stack traces on the machine you can SSH into. A checkout request may touch a dozen services, queue consumers, and cache layers while returning only a generic HTTP 503. CPU dashboards from the SRE monitoring chapter detect that something hurts; they rarely reveal which dependency slipped. Practitioner experience—articulated sharply by Charity Majors and the Honeycomb team—showed that metrics surface the pain, logs explain individual decisions, and traces show the cross-service story. OpenTelemetry exists so teams can invest once in instrumentation and still choose or change Grafana, Prometheus, or commercial backends without rewriting applications.

When to use

  • Any production service that owns customer-facing SLOs should emit RED-style metrics to Prometheus, structured logs, and distributed traces at least on critical paths.
  • Microservice or modular monolith architectures where latency and errors cross network boundaries—traces are not optional luxury telemetry.
  • Organizations adopting error budgets, multi-window burn-rate alerts, and blameless postmortems described in the SRE book—those practices assume metric-backed SLIs plus investigatory logs and traces.
  • Teams building a Grafana-centric stack who want Explore workflows that pivot from metrics exemplars to Tempo traces to Loki lines keyed on trace_id.

When not to use

  • Do not attempt to store every request at full fidelity in all three pillars simultaneously at extreme traffic—cost and cardinality explode. Use Prometheus for aggregates, tail-based trace sampling in the OTel Collector, and log level plus sampling discipline.
  • Do not treat the three pillars as a substitute for defining SLOs and alerts; charts without ownership still page nobody usefully.

Typical architecture

Instrumented workloads export OTLP to an OpenTelemetry Collector that fans out to metric, trace, and log backends; Grafana ties the signals together for investigation.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Each signal covers blind spots in the others: Prometheus metrics show fleet-wide regression in seconds; Loki logs retain business context; traces localize cross-service delay.
  • OpenTelemetry decouples instrumentation from vendor roadmaps—export to Prometheus today and add Tempo tomorrow without forking application code.
  • Shared trace_id fields knit Grafana Explore into a single narrative from burn-rate alert to slow span to explanatory log line—far faster than unstructured search.

Cons

  • Standing up all three pillars with correlation is a platform project: SDKs, Collectors, Prometheus HA, Loki retention, trace stores, and Grafana datasource wiring.
  • Prometheus metric labels tempt teams to encode unbounded dimensions—user_id as a label can create millions of series and violates SRE monitoring guidance on cardinality.
  • Traces break when context propagation stops at queues, thread pools, or batch jobs unless producers and consumers honor W3C trace headers and OTel baggage.

Implementation notes

Start with OpenTelemetry auto-instrumentation for HTTP, gRPC, and database clients, then add manual spans and log fields for domain attributes the auto layer cannot know. Run a Collector tier that batches, enriches with Kubernetes metadata, and applies tail-based sampling so error and high-latency traces are kept while happy paths sample down. Export metrics to Prometheus (or remote write to Mimir), traces to Tempo, logs to Loki—Grafana datasources then share labels like service and environment.

Enforce structured JSON logs with OTel-injected trace_id and span_id on every request path. Enable Prometheus exemplars where supported so dashboard users jump into traces. Document RED metrics per service and align alert rules with SLO chapters from the SRE book rather than paging on raw counter thresholds. Review cardinality weekly with prometheus_tsdb_head_series and Loki stream counts.

Beyond three silos: Charity Majors argues the pillars model encourages thin, disconnected signals—metrics without request context, logs without traces. Her “wide event” pattern emits one rich record per request (latency, user, error, key attributes) queryable without stitching three backends. Whether you use Honeycomb-style events or Grafana’s three-store stack, the lesson is the same: preserve high-cardinality context in the right pillar (trace span or log field), not Prometheus labels.

Profiling and continuous profiling (see Profiling) act as a fourth signal for code-level regressions. Metrics answer what changed; traces answer where; logs answer why in narrative; profiles answer which function.

Common failure modes

  • Monitoring without investigatory signals: Dashboards and alerts exist, but unstructured logs and missing traces leave on-call guessing during novel failures—the gap Charity Majors highlights between tools and observability.
  • Cardinality in the wrong pillar: Request IDs as Prometheus labels explode TSDB; keep high cardinality in traces and log fields, not metric labels.
  • Broken trace chains: Message consumers that do not extract trace context produce orphan spans and false confidence that a path is healthy.
  • Uniform trace sampling: Probabilistic head sampling at 1% discards rare errors; tail sampling in the Collector preserves errors and slow traces while controlling cost.

Decision checklist

  • Are metrics, structured logs, and traces instrumented for customer-critical paths?
  • Does OpenTelemetry standardize production instrumentation and export?
  • Do log records include trace and span identifiers whenever context is active?
  • Does trace sampling retain errors and high-latency requests?
  • Is context propagated through queues, workflows, and background jobs?
  • Can on-call pivot in Grafana from Prometheus alerts to Tempo and Loki without custom scripts?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — checkout latency: A Prometheus alert on elevated p99 latency leads through an exemplar to a Tempo trace showing delay in a downstream gRPC call; Loki logs with the same trace_id reveal connection pool exhaustion.
  • Illustrative scenario — greenfield microservices: New services ship with OTel from day one; a single Grafana folder tracks RED metrics, log volume, and trace error rate per deployment.
  • Case study — Google SRE monitoring practice: The SRE book’s Golden Signals and SLI discipline anchor metric design while logs and traces remain the tools for explaining unexpected deviations from those SLIs.
