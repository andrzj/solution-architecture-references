Alerting Strategies

Google SRE alerting philosophy: page humans only when urgent, actionable, and novel; prefer symptom over cause; use multi-window SLO burn rates; route and inhibit with Alertmanager and on-call tools.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

Alerting is the subset of monitoring that notifies a human to take action now. Google’s SRE book draws a sharp line: monitoring includes dashboards, exploratory graphs, and log analysis; alerting is only for conditions where a prompt human response will materially reduce user impact or data loss. Everything else belongs in tickets, dashboards, or automated remediation — not on a pager.

A paging alert should satisfy three properties — urgent (waiting until tomorrow harms users or SLOs), actionable (the responder can do something concrete in the short term), and novel (automation is not already handling it, and the same condition is not already an open incident). Alerts that fail any of these tests become noise.

Symptom-based rules fire on user-visible degradation: elevated error ratio, latency above SLO, failed synthetic journey. Cause-based rules fire on internal precursors: disk filling, certificate expiry, queue depth. The monitoring chapter recommends paging primarily on symptoms tied to the Four Golden Signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation). Cause-based signals are still valuable — as capacity tickets, daily emails, or dashboard annotations — but paging on causes trains on-call to ignore pages when users are unaffected.

SLO burn-rate alerting (detailed in the Google SRE Workbook) compares your current error ratio to the error budget you are allowed to spend over the SLO window. For a 30-day 99.9% availability target, you may “spend” only 0.1% of requests as errors across the month—about 43 minutes of equivalent downtime if errors were concentrated. A burn rate of 1× means you are consuming budget exactly on pace to exhaust it at month end; 14× means you would burn the entire monthly budget in roughly two days if the present error rate continued. The workbook’s fast burn alert therefore fires when both a one-hour window and a five-minute window show error ratios above 14 times the allowed steady-state error rate (for 99.9%, that is 14 × 0.001). Requiring both windows filters single-scrape blips: a five-minute spike alone might be deploy noise, while a one-hour elevation alone might lag a sudden total outage; together they mean sustained user-visible harm worth waking someone. A slower burn (for example 6× over six hours confirmed on thirty-minute windows) pages with lower urgency before the month is lost to gradual drift.

Alert fatigue is the predictable result of violating these rules: hundreds of low-value pages, desensitised responders, and slower response when a genuine outage arrives.

Why it exists

Distributed systems generate endless metric anomalies; humans do not scale. The SRE approach treats on-call time as a scarce resource: every page should save user-observable reliability that automation cannot. Teams that page on every threshold breach experience documented alarm fatigue — responders mute channels, incidents are detected via customer complaints, and attrition rises. Symptom- and SLO-based design collapses page volume while improving detection of real budget burn.

Alertmanager-style routing (grouping, inhibition, silences) exists because failures cascade: one root symptom should produce one coordinated page, not fifty copies as each dependency retries. Runbooks linked from alert annotations close the loop between “something fired” and “here is what to try first.”

When to use

  • Any service with an on-call rotation — define explicit severity levels and which severities page versus ticket.
  • Services with published or internal SLOs — implement multi-window burn-rate rules per critical SLI.
  • After mergers or dashboard sprawl — run an alert audit: delete or downgrade rules that never required human action in 30 days.
  • Post-incident hardening — add symptom alerts and runbooks for gaps exposed in review, not only more cause thresholds.

When not to use

  • Paging on metrics with no runbook and no recent successful drill — if responders routinely shrug, demote or delete the rule.
  • Using static error-rate thresholds when SLOs exist — burn rate incorporates budget context; fixed 1% pages at the wrong times.
  • Alerting on logs at high volume without sampling — prefer metric recording rules or log-based metrics with tight cardinality.

Typical architecture

Evaluation happens in the metrics tier; Alertmanager (or equivalent) deduplicates, routes by severity, and hands off to on-call tooling.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Burn-rate pages track error-budget consumption — prioritisation follows user impact, not whichever threshold was guessed first.
  • Multi-window AND conditions filter single-scrape spikes while still catching sustained degradation.
  • Symptom paging plus ticketed cause alerts preserve capacity foresight without waking people for healthy-looking services.
  • Grouped alerts and inhibition keep one incident to one coordinated notification.

Cons

  • Burn-rate rules require trustworthy SLIs — garbage numerators produce confident wrong pages.
  • Mis-tuned fast-burn multipliers miss brief total outages; tune short windows for critical journeys.
  • Routing trees rot as teams reorganise — stale services page former employees.
  • Over-reliance on automation to “fix” alerts without deleting rules recreates fatigue under new names.

Implementation notes

Run a quarterly alert audit: export firing history, mark each rule as actionable or not, delete or downgrade the rest. Google’s guidance is blunt — if a page does not regularly require human intervention, remove it. Record SLI error ratios as Prometheus (or equivalent) recording rules before writing burn expressions so alert YAML stays readable.

Encode burn tiers as recording rules plus PromQL that mirrors the workbook tables: numerator and denominator must be the same SLI queries your executives see on the error-budget dashboard, not a hand-tuned error counter from one pod. Attach runbook_url and deep-link dashboard annotations to every page, and add a for: duration on volatile metrics so a single failed scrape does not wake anyone.

In Alertmanager, route pages by severity and owning team, and define inhibit_rules so a parent symptom—fast SLO burn or synthetic journey failure on checkout—suppresses dependent cause alerts on the same service label (CPU high, pod restart loop). That keeps one incident to one notification tree. Review receivers in Git and rehearse with amtool before changing on-call rotations.

Measure alert quality the same way you measure service quality: track pages per engineer per week, what fraction required a human mitigating action within the SLO response window, mean time to acknowledge, and how often the same rule refires within twenty-four hours. Google’s workbook treats alerts that never change operator behavior as defects—delete or demote them. Reserve paging for conditions where waiting until morning would miss the error budget or harm users; a disk at seventy percent with flat latency belongs in a ticket, while two consecutive synthetic checkout failures belong on the pager.

Common failure modes

  • Paging on saturation alone: CPU at 90% with flat latency and error rate is a ticket, not a 03:00 page — pair saturation with symptom signals.
  • Missing runbooks: Alerts without links force rediscovery during incidents; treat missing runbooks as alert misconfiguration.
  • Alert storms: No grouping/inhibition — one dependency failure pages every downstream service.
  • Flapping: No for window or hysteresis on metrics that oscillate at the threshold.
  • Duplicate paths: Grafana alerts and Prometheus alerts on the same condition with different thresholds — consolidate evaluation.

Decision checklist

  • Does every paging rule map to a user-visible symptom or SLO burn rate?
  • Is each page urgent, actionable, and novel under the SRE definition?
  • Are multi-window burn-rate alerts live for top user journeys?
  • Do all pages link to current runbooks and dashboards?
  • Are inhibition rules tested for cascading failure scenarios?
  • Has alert volume per on-call shift been reviewed in the last quarter?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — A team deletes 120 cause-based pages and keeps 18 symptom and burn-rate rules; mean pages per week drop from 180 to 12; MTTR on real outages improves because responders treat pages seriously.
  • Illustrative scenario — Slow 0.4% error rate from a degraded dependency fires a 3× burn ticket before any customer escalation; rollback completes with 40% of monthly budget remaining.
  • Case study — Google’s SRE Workbook documents multi-window burn alerting as the default for SLO-backed services, replacing static error-ratio thresholds.
