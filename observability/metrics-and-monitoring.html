Metrics and Monitoring

Metrics and monitoring translate production behavior into Prometheus time series, Grafana visualization, and alert policies grounded in RED, USE, and the Google SRE book’s Four Golden Signals.

⏱ 11 min read

What it is

Metrics are numeric measurements recorded over time—counters of work done, gauges of current state, and histograms of latency distributions—that let teams see how a system behaves in aggregate. Monitoring is the operational practice of collecting those measurements continuously, visualizing them in dashboards such as Grafana, and firing alerts when symptom-based or SLO-based thresholds breach. In cloud-native stacks, Prometheus is the de facto metrics engine: it scrapes HTTP /metrics endpoints on a schedule, stores samples in its TSDB, and evaluates PromQL for alerts and graphs.

Prometheus exposes four instrument types. A counter only increases (total requests, total errors) and is queried with rate(). A gauge moves up or down (queue depth, memory used). A histogram buckets observations so you can approximate percentiles with histogram_quantile()—prefer histograms over summaries in distributed systems because summaries do not aggregate cleanly across pods. Each series is identified by metric name plus labels; cardinality is the count of unique label combinations—the resource Honeycomb and Prometheus operators alike warn you not to explode with unbounded dimensions.

Methodologies keep monitoring focused. RED (rate, errors, duration) suits services. USE (utilization, saturation, errors) suits nodes and disks. The Google SRE book’s Four Golden Signals—latency, traffic, errors, saturation—anchor production dashboards and SLI design. OpenTelemetry can emit Prometheus-compatible metrics via OTLP and the Collector, so instrumentation stays aligned with traces and logs in the three pillars model.

Why it exists

Logs are too slow and too voluminous to be the first detector for fleet-wide regressions; traces are too expensive to retain for every request at full fidelity. Metrics compress behavior into numbers you can retain for months and query in milliseconds—exactly what SRE error budgets and paging policies require. Prometheus’s pull model also encodes uptime: a missing scrape is itself a signal that a target vanished, which matches ephemeral Kubernetes pods that come and go faster than humans notice.

Monitoring without methodology devolves into chart sprawl. RED, USE, and Golden Signals exist so teams instrument the smallest sufficient set and spend investigative energy on logs and traces only after metrics prove something changed.

When to use

  • Every production service exposing RED metrics on its HTTP, gRPC, or message handlers.
  • Infrastructure tiers instrumented with USE metrics via node exporters, kube-state-metrics, or cloud provider integrations scraped into Prometheus.
  • SLO programs that translate user journeys into SLI metrics and multi-window burn-rate alerts—as described in the SRE workbook chapters on alerting.
  • Grafana dashboards shared with product and leadership for traffic, error, and latency trends—not as vanity charts, but as agreed operational views.

When not to use

  • Do not store per-request identifiers or unbounded label values in Prometheus; put that detail in OpenTelemetry traces and structured Loki logs.
  • Do not alert on raw cumulative counters without rate()—you will page forever on the first error in history.

Typical architecture

Prometheus scrapes instrumented targets, stores time series locally or via remote write, and Grafana renders dashboards and alert rules on top.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Metrics compress enormous traffic into storable time series—ideal for months of retention and sub-second Grafana queries during incidents.
  • Prometheus scrape failures surface missing targets immediately, complementing application-level health checks.
  • PromQL and recording rules express SLIs, error budgets, and SRE burn rates without re-instrumenting applications for every new question.
  • OpenTelemetry metrics export keeps counters and histograms consistent with traces when routed through the Collector.

Cons

  • Histogram percentiles are only as good as bucket boundaries—misconfigured buckets lie smoothly in dashboards.
  • Single-node Prometheus lacks HA and long retention; production needs Thanos, Mimir, or remote write planning from the start.
  • Pull-based discovery grows complex across many clusters; federation and relabel rules become their own operational domain.
  • Metrics alone cannot explain novel failures—Charity Majors’ observability argument still applies: you need logs and traces after metrics point.

Implementation notes

Instrument HTTP and RPC layers with RED: http_requests_total labeled by coarse route template, status class, and method; histograms for duration with buckets matching your SLO threshold. Export OpenTelemetry metrics to Prometheus via the Collector’s Prometheus remote write or a scrape endpoint. Define recording rules for every alert and heavy dashboard query—name them job:http_requests:rate5m style so dependencies are obvious.

Run cardinality reviews weekly: top series by label job, drop toxic labels at scrape time with metric_relabel_configs, and never put user or request IDs on labels. Pair Grafana panels with exemplars linking to Tempo when possible. For long-term trends, remote-write to Mimir or Thanos while keeping local Prometheus for fast alerting. Document Golden Signal dashboards per tier—edge, service, data—and tie pages to user impact, not CPU trivia.

Operationalize the Google four golden signals on one dashboard row per service: request rate as traffic, success ratio and 5xx rate as errors, histogram percentiles as latency, and queue depth or thread pool rejections as saturation. The RED method is the microservice translation of that idea: rate of requests, errors as a fraction of requests, and duration histograms for each handler. The USE method applies to resources underneath the service: utilization (how busy the device is), saturation (work waiting because the device is busy), and errors on the resource. A host can show ninety percent CPU utilization while saturation is low because threads block on I/O; pairing CPU with run-queue length or disk await time tells you whether you are capacity-bound. Page on latency and error budget burn first; use saturation panels to explain why and to schedule capacity work, not as the primary pager unless user-visible symptoms are already degrading.

Common failure modes

  • Cardinality explosion: A new label with unbounded values multiplies series count until Prometheus OOMs—treat it like a production incident prevention task.
  • Wrong histogram buckets: Default second-scale buckets make sub-200ms APIs look fine while p99 lies; tune per service SLO.
  • Alerting on raw counters: Pages that never resolve because totals only increase—always use rate() or recording rules.
  • Dashboard-only monitoring: Charts nobody owns and alerts nobody trusts—violates SRE guidance to automate symptom detection.

Decision checklist

  • Does every service expose RED metrics scraped into Prometheus?
  • Do infrastructure tiers expose USE metrics for compute, storage, and network?
  • Are label cardinality limits documented and enforced at scrape time?
  • Do recording rules back every production alert and heavy Grafana query?
  • Is long-term storage (Thanos/Mimir) configured for capacity and SLO history?
  • Do histogram buckets match each service’s latency SLO and traffic shape?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — API gateway: RED metrics per route template power a Grafana board; when error ratio crosses an SLO, exemplars jump to Tempo traces while Loki logs supply error codes.
  • Illustrative scenario — datastore saturation: USE metrics from a database exporter alert on connection pool utilization and replication lag before queries time out in applications.
  • Case study — Google SRE Golden Signals: Production monitoring at Google popularized latency, traffic, errors, and saturation as the minimal service health vocabulary—mapped directly to Prometheus SLI recording rules today.
