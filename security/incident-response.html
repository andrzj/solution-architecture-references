Security Architecture

Incident Response Architecture

Technical architecture for incident response: the IR lifecycle, SIEM integration, automated runbooks, evidence preservation, blast radius containment, and post-incident review processes.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

Incident response (IR) architecture turns detect–triage–contain–eradicate–recover into repeatable playbooks instead of ad-hoc war rooms. NIST SP 800-61 defines preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, and post-incident review—each phase has tooling equivalents (runbooks, SIEM rules, SOAR actions, backups, retrospectives). Platforms include Splunk, Elastic Security, Microsoft Sentinel, and cloud-native aggregators (Security Hub, Security Command Center).

IR is not only tooling—roles (incident commander, scribe, comms), severity definitions, legal notification paths, and tabletop exercises determine whether detections become controlled incidents or unbounded crises. The technical stack centres on a SIEM that ingests, normalises, and correlates logs (Splunk, Elastic Security, Microsoft Sentinel, AWS Security Hub). SOAR enriches alerts with threat intel (VirusTotal, Shodan), runs approved containment (block IP, revoke sessions), and escalates judgement calls to humans. Preserve evidence—memory dumps, disk images, packet captures, log exports to WORM buckets—before eradication so investigators and counsel retain chain of custody. Blast-radius containment limits spread via segmentation, least-privilege IAM, and immutable infrastructure patterns.

Why it exists

Breaches are inevitable; dwell time and data loss depend on preparation. Without centralised logs, runbooks, and practised communications, teams destroy evidence while rebooting servers. GDPR breach-notification clocks, PCI, and HIPAA impose timelines that require pre-staged contacts and decision trees. IBM research links tested IR programmes to roughly 54 faster days to containment and about $2.66M lower breach cost versus organisations without formal IR. Evidence matters for root-cause analysis and for litigation if prosecution follows. IR architecture converts chaotic events into measured procedures with auditable timelines.

When to use

  • All production environments processing customer, financial, or health data.
  • Every organisation operating production systems—a baseline IR plan and central logging are minimum expectations.
  • Regulated sectors (financial, healthcare, critical infrastructure) where ISO 27001, SOC 2, or PCI DSS mandate formal incident handling.
  • Organisations with SOC 2, ISO 27001, or cyber insurance requiring documented IR plans.
  • High-value IP or customer data targeted by ransomware or nation-state actors.
  • Multi-cloud estates where unified detection reduces blind spots.
  • Teams running purple-team or red-team exercises to validate controls and runbooks.

When not to use

  • Do not defer runbooks until a “mature SIEM” exists—start with logging baselines and named roles.
  • Avoid unattended auto-containment on business-critical systems until playbooks are tested and rollback paths exist.
  • Scale plan depth to organisation size—a startup needs a concise runbook and log aggregation; an enterprise needs SOC coverage and SOAR integration.

Typical architecture

Telemetry flows into a central SIEM; playbooks drive containment via identity revocation, network isolation, and evidence preservation.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Pre-approved actions shrink mean time to contain.
  • Immutable logs preserve evidence for legal and insurer review.
  • SOAR automates enrichment and ticketing, reducing analyst toil.
  • Tabletops surface gaps before real attackers exploit them.
  • Automated playbooks act in seconds where manual tool-hopping takes minutes under stress.
  • Cross-log correlation reveals attacks invisible in a single data source.

Cons

  • Alert fatigue persists without tuning and tier-1 triage discipline.
  • Over-automation can isolate the wrong hosts during false positives.
  • Evidence preservation needs storage, tooling, and rehearsal before incidents.
  • Cross-border notification rules complicate global incidents.
  • Retention and PII in logs create secondary compliance obligations.
  • Readiness decays without recurring game days and updated contacts.

Implementation notes

Aggregate cloud control-plane, IdP, endpoint, and application logs into a security account with WORM storage. Define a severity matrix tied to customer impact and regulatory clocks. Maintain runbooks per scenario (credential stuffing, ransomware, insider export) with steps that preserve forensic images before destructive actions.

Write runbooks before you need them; store copies outside the SIEM so they remain reachable if primary systems are suspect. Use out-of-band channels (separate chat or call bridge) for IR coordination. Integrate ticketing, status pages, and executive templates. Run quarterly tabletops and annual retainer drills; publish blameless postmortems with tracked remediations.

Adopt hypothesis-based detections mapped to MITRE ATT&CK techniques relevant to your threat model. Favour high-fidelity, low-volume rules over noisy vendor defaults.

Each runbook should list detection indicators, containment steps that preserve disk and memory images, evidence to export, escalation paths, and customer notification templates. Integrate SOAR with ticketing and asset inventory so analysts see owner, data classification, and blast radius on first screen. After incidents, run blameless postmortems with tracked remediation items linked to control frameworks.

Ship structured application security logs—authentication success and failure, authorisation denial, privilege elevation, configuration drift—to the SIEM alongside CloudTrail, VPC Flow Logs, EDR telemetry, DNS logs, and IdP sign-in events. Store security-account copies in WORM buckets with retention that meets legal hold requirements. Define customer notification and regulator clocks in the severity matrix so legal can act without debating timelines during an active breach.

Classify incidents (P1 active breach, P2 credential abuse, P3 policy violation) with response SLAs and executive notification rules. Automate WAF blocks and session revocation only where rollback is documented and tested.

Common failure modes

  • Reimaging without forensics—evidence lost before root cause analysis.
  • Silosed logs—compromised admin activity cannot be correlated across accounts.
  • No comms owner—customers learn from social media first.
  • Untested backups—ransomware recovery fails during crisis.
  • Stale playbooks—architecture changed but runbooks reference retired tools.
  • Premature closure—lateral movement undiscovered before sign-off.
  • No tested runbooks—paper plans that were never exercised slow real responses.
  • Evidence destroyed before preservation—terminating VMs before snapshots eliminates scope analysis.
  • In-band IR chat—compromised IdP adversaries monitor the same Slack used to coordinate response.

Decision checklist

  • Is there a published IR plan with roles and a 24/7 contact tree?
  • Are security logs centralised with tamper protection and tested retention?
  • Do runbooks cover top threat scenarios with containment options?
  • Was a tabletop conducted in the last 12 months with executive participation?
  • Are legal and privacy notification paths defined with draft templates?
  • Does SOAR or runbooks integrate with ticketing and asset inventory?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — cloud credential leak: CloudTrail anomaly triggers session revocation; incident commander coordinates customer comms within SLA.
  • Credential stuffing response: GuardDuty triggers a SOAR playbook that enriches IPs, blocks WAF rules, forces MFA refresh, opens a ticket, and pages an analyst if more than ten accounts are hit.
  • Ransomware isolation: EDR detects encryption activity; host quarantined; EBS snapshotted; CloudTrail and app logs copied to WORM storage; P1 bridge opened.
  • S3 data exposure: Config detects a public bucket; automation makes it private, preserves access logs, mines CloudTrail for 90-day object access to size notification obligations.
