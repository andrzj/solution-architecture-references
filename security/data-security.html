Security Architecture

Data Security

Protecting data at rest, in transit, and in use: data classification frameworks, column-level encryption, tokenisation, data masking, PII handling, GDPR/CCPA technical controls, and access auditing.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

Data security protects information through its lifecycle—discovery, classification, access control, encryption, masking, retention, and disposal. Governance defines who may use which datasets; technical controls (column encryption, tokenisation, DLP, row-level security) preserve confidentiality when configuration drifts or insiders abuse access.

Data classification tiers (Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted) drive proportional safeguards. Encryption at rest defends stolen media or bucket snapshots; encryption in transit (TLS) protects paths on the wire; column-level encryption (application- or field-level) shields crown-jewel attributes even when the database role is legitimate. Tokenisation swaps PANs or national IDs for vault-backed surrogates that cannot be reversed without the vault—unlike encryption where key plus ciphertext might be attacked offline. Masking feeds lower-trust environments: static masking builds de-identified test copies; dynamic masking redacts query results for unprivileged analysts. GDPR and CCPA add minimisation, purpose limitation, retention limits, and erasure mechanics mapped in the NIST Privacy Framework.

Why it exists

Exfiltrated customer records drive the highest breach costs—IBM’s 2023 Cost of a Data Breach Report cited an average of $4.45M per incident—and trigger fines under GDPR, HIPAA, and state privacy laws (GDPR penalties up to €20M or 4% of global revenue). Encrypting everything without classification wastes budget and blocks analytics; intentional tiering proves to regulators that exposure was designed, not accidental. Platform encryption protects stolen backups and buckets; column-level encryption means SQL injection against crown-jewel columns returns ciphertext. NIST SP 800-111 guides storage encryption choices auditors expect to see documented.

Cloud data stores (RDS, S3, BigQuery) provide baseline encryption; architects still label sensitivity in the data catalog, apply field controls for regulated attributes, and align retention with legal holds and erasure requests. Uniform catalog labels, access reviews, and immutable query logs supply the evidence trail privacy and financial regulators request during examinations.

When to use

  • Systems storing PII, PHI, PCI data, or trade secrets—encryption, access logging, and erasure capability are often legally required.
  • Payment card environments where PCI DSS mandates tokenisation or encryption of stored card data.
  • Healthcare workloads where HIPAA expects PHI encrypted at rest and in transit.
  • Data lakes and warehouses combining multiple sources without column policies.
  • Shared dev or staging accounts—use masked or synthetic data, never raw production copies.
  • Multi-tenant SaaS requiring logical isolation per customer dataset.
  • Cross-border processing subject to residency, transfer mechanisms, or sector rules.

When not to use

  • Do not field-encrypt every column—reserve application-layer encryption for genuinely sensitive attributes where index trade-offs are acceptable.
  • Column-level encryption on all fields breaks database-side filters, aggregates, and indexes; restrict to SSN, PAN, password hashes, and medical record numbers where the performance cost is justified.
  • Do not tokenise or encrypt operational metrics with no sensitivity—focus budget on classified tiers.
  • Avoid copying production snapshots to dev; use masked, synthetic, or subset data instead.

Typical architecture

Classification metadata in the catalog drives encryption depth, masking pipelines for non-production, and monitored export paths across stores.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Classification focuses spend on the strongest controls where harm is highest.
  • Tokenisation shrinks PCI and PHI scope inside application databases.
  • DLP and immutable audit trails deter insider exfiltration.
  • Automated retention supports erasure requests and litigation holds.
  • Tokenisation removes PAN from application databases, shrinking PCI DSS assessment scope.
  • Masking eliminates engineer exposure to live PII during feature development.

Cons

  • Field encryption complicates predicates—WHERE ssn = ? may require full scans or app-side decrypt.
  • Column encryption demands KMS integration, rotation runbooks, and re-encryption projects.
  • Erasure in event-sourced systems often needs crypto-shredding instead of physical deletes.
  • Unlabelled tables in the catalog bypass policies silently.
  • DLP false positives interrupt legitimate analyst workflows.
  • Masking tools may break referential integrity needed for integration tests.
  • Global residency rules fragment architecture and operations.

Implementation notes

Discover and label data in the catalog; propagate tags to IAM and query engines. Enable platform encryption by default; add envelope encryption for restricted columns using KMS. Mask or tokenise non-production; automate anonymisation jobs.

Use per-record DEKs wrapped by a KMS KEK so rotation re-wraps keys instead of re-encrypting all rows. For equality lookups on encrypted fields, evaluate deterministic encryption or blind indexes with eyes open to information leakage. Design erasure around stable internal user IDs; for append-only streams, crypto-shred per-user keys in KMS when deletion is requested.

Define response DTOs for regulated exports so APIs cannot leak hidden columns. Quarterly access reviews on warehouse roles and backup operators should compare grants to catalog labels. Wire DLP on email and chat when analysts handle restricted extracts. Test erasure and retention jobs annually so privacy commitments stay executable, not aspirational.

Enforce row-level security or ABAC in analytics tools. Monitor bulk exports with UEBA. Document retention schedules and legal-hold overrides where legal and engineering teams can access them during incidents.

Start discovery and classification before buying more encryption SKUs—tags should flow into IAM, lakehouse engines, and backup policies. Enable platform encryption everywhere; reserve envelope encryption with KMS for restricted columns. Mask or synthesise non-production refresh jobs; never restore raw production snapshots to laptops.

Common failure modes

  • Replica sprawl—ML copies and backups lack the same controls as primary stores.
  • Over-privileged analysts—warehouse roles query raw PII without masking.
  • Keys beside ciphertext—DEKs or master keys stored with data nullify encryption.
  • Stale classification—new columns default to Internal without review.
  • Erasure without cascades—privacy jobs miss derived tables, search indexes, or events.
  • Plaintext backups—TDE-protected databases exported to unencrypted object storage.
  • PII in application logs—HTTP body logging captures emails and names with weaker retention controls than the primary database.
  • Retention on paper only—policies promise deletion but scheduled jobs never run.
  • No stream erasure plan—Kafka topics without per-user keys make GDPR deletion impractical.

Decision checklist

  • Is every production datastore classified with owner and sensitivity tier?
  • Are restricted fields encrypted or tokenised with KMS-backed keys?
  • Is non-production data masked or synthetic?
  • Do access reviews cover analytics and backup roles?
  • Are export and bulk-query paths monitored?
  • Are retention and erasure procedures tested annually?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — PCI scope reduction: Card numbers tokenised at the edge; only tokens in the app DB; PAN vault isolated in the CDE.
  • Healthcare patient records: PHI encrypted per column with KMS; per-patient encrypted DEKs; only authorised service accounts may decrypt; PHI reads logged to immutable audit trails.
  • E-commerce checkout: Stripe or Braintree vault holds PAN; the app stores token plus last four digits; PCI scope shrinks to integration points.
  • GDPR erasure at scale: User deletion nulls PII, retains pseudonymous user_id in analytics, crypto-shreds per-user keys for Kafka payloads, and logs completion timestamps for regulators.
