CAP Theorem

You can only guarantee two of Consistency, Availability, and Partition Tolerance — understanding the fundamental constraint of distributed data systems.

⏱ 7 min read

What it is

CAP is the shorthand for a constraint on distributed data stores: you cannot simultaneously promise that every read is up to date (consistency), that every request gets a response (availability), and that the system keeps working when the network fails (partition tolerance). Partitions happen in real deployments, so the practical fork is what you do when nodes cannot talk: refuse some requests (CP) or keep answering with data that might be stale (AP).

A regional shopping cart can lean AP—users keep adding items even if replicas lag—while capturing payment often needs CP behavior so two nodes never disagree about whether money moved.

In CAP, consistency means linearizability: every client observes a single ordered history of operations, as if one copy of the data existed. Weaker models—causal, read-your-writes, eventual—still matter in production; CAP names the strongest guarantee you cannot keep alongside full availability when the network partitions.

Why it exists

Before CAP was widely understood, teams treated distributed databases like bigger single-node databases: add replication, hope the network cooperates, and assume strong consistency plus high availability. Eric Brewer's formulation at PODC 2000, later formalized by Gilbert and Lynch, made the limit explicit. The question stops being "which database is fastest?" and becomes "what must this data do when the network splits?"

In the formal proof, consistency means every read receives the most recent write (or an error)—in practice, architects map this to linearizability, where operations appear to execute in a single global order. Availability means every non-failing node returns a response within a finite time, with no requirement that the data be current. Partition tolerance means the system continues to operate despite arbitrary message loss between nodes. Because partitions occur in any real multi-node deployment, the operative choice during a partition is CP (sacrifice availability on the minority side or reject stale reads) versus AP (keep answering with possibly stale local state).

CAP also surfaces a recurring production surprise: picking a store marketed as "consistent" and "always on," then learning that under partition either writes stall or reads return old values. Naming CP vs AP behavior up front forces failure-mode design, not just happy-path throughput.

Partitions are not rare edge cases. Switch failures, misconfigured security groups, AZ brownouts, and rolling deploys that isolate quorum members all create split-brain windows. Architects who plan only for the happy path discover CAP during incidents when writes succeed on two sides of a bridge.

When to use

  • Use CAP when you narrow database candidates for a multi-node or multi-region system.
  • Use it when you write down consistency requirements per use case, because ledger lines and like counts do not belong in the same bucket.
  • Use it when you define partition behavior — error, degrade, or deliberately read stale data.
  • Use it when you explain to stakeholders why “always consistent and always available” is not a checkbox feature.
  • Use it when you design failover: promoted secondaries, stretched clusters, and multi-region active-active all assume partition behavior you must name.

When not to use

  • Do not rely on CAP alone, because it ignores read-your-writes, causal consistency, and other middle ground; pair it with PACELC for latency trade-offs.
  • Do not apply CAP to single-node databases, because partition tolerance only matters once independent failure domains exist.
  • Do not treat CP or AP labels as a substitute for chaos tests on your actual topology and failure modes.

Pros and cons

Pros of understanding CAP

  • Surfaces consistency needs before the datastore is chosen, not after the first split-brain incident.
  • Sets realistic expectations when marketing slides promise "fully consistent, always online" globally.
  • Gives architects and product owners a shared vocabulary for partition trade-offs.
  • Encourages documented partition protocols instead of improvised on-call behavior.

Limitations of CAP

  • Only two coarse buckets—real systems expose a spectrum of consistency levels per operation.
  • Describes behavior during partitions, not steady-state latency (PACELC covers that gap).
  • Uses linearizability for "C," which is stricter than many business rules actually need.
  • Often reduced to vendor slogans ("pick two") without tying choices to concrete failure scenarios.

Implementation notes

CP systems: During a partition, isolated minorities stop accepting writes (and sometimes reads) rather than serve data that might be wrong. ZooKeeper and etcd use consensus (Zab and Raft) so acknowledged writes are agreed cluster-wide; minority sides go quiet. Reach for these when correctness beats brief outage—locks, leader election, cluster config.

AP systems: Every reachable replica keeps serving traffic; replicas may diverge until replication catches up. Cassandra's tunable levels (ONE, QUORUM, ALL) and DynamoDB's default AP posture target feeds, carts, and preferences where availability and latency matter more than strict global agreement.

Tunable consistency: Many stores let you raise consistency per read or write. QUORUM/QUORUM in Cassandra approximates strong behavior at higher latency; ONE favors speed and accepts stale reads. The CAP label is a default posture, not a fixed setting—document the level you use for each critical path.

CA and operations: Single-node PostgreSQL or a co-located primary/replica pair can be strongly consistent and highly available until the network splits independent failure domains. Health checks and client routing must match that reality: promoting a replica without fencing the old primary recreates partition ambiguity even on “CA” hardware.

Per-operation knobs: DynamoDB offers strongly consistent reads as an opt-in on individual GetItem/Query calls (at roughly double read cost and higher latency). Cassandra’s consistency level is per statement—document ONE vs QUORUM on checkout paths, not one label for the whole cluster. CockroachDB and Google Spanner expose serializable or external consistency without forcing a single CAP sticker.

Detection and chaos: Inject latency and black-hole faults between AZs in staging; measure stale reads, write rejection rates, and client retry amplification. Metrics: replica lag histograms, fencing token violations, “read served from stale replica” counters if the client library exposes them.

Partition tolerance is not optional: Once you replicate across processes, racks, or regions, the network can drop or delay messages. “CA” (consistent and available without partition tolerance) describes a single synchronous process or a cluster that shares fate with one failure domain—not a geo-distributed database. Marketing “CA plus multi-master” without naming minority behavior is how teams discover CAP during the first regional outage.

Linearizability vs serializability: Linearizability orders every operation against real-time: a read that completes after a write must see that write’s effect. Serializability (common in SQL) orders transactions into some equivalent serial schedule but does not require that schedule to match wall-clock order across clients—fine for ledger batches, insufficient when a mobile client must immediately read its own payment confirmation. CAP’s “C” is linearizability, stricter than many OLTP “strong” modes.

Brewer’s 2012 revision: In CAP Twelve Years Later, Brewer argues teams should pair coarse CP/AP thinking with operation-level policies, timeouts, and “proof by test” under partitions—not choose logos once in a slide deck.

Kleppmann’s critique: Labeling a whole product “CP” or “AP” hides per-request consistency levels (Cassandra CL, DynamoDB consistent reads, Postgres session settings). Kleppmann recommends describing what each API path does on failure and on stale read instead of a single triangle sticker—then follow with PACELC for healthy-network latency trade-offs.

Jepsen and evidence: Kyle Kingsbury’s Jepsen analyses partition real databases and document lost writes, stale reads, and ambiguous leader election. Treat vendor consistency claims as hypotheses until your topology, consistency level, and failure injection match production—or cite a Jepsen report for your exact version when available.

When NOT to invoke CAP: A single-region PostgreSQL primary with one synchronous standby is often simpler than distributed NoSQL if traffic fits one node. CAP applies when independent failure domains can diverge; it does not replace normal capacity planning, index design, or backup RPO/RTO work.

Common failure modes

  • AP store, CP expectations: Running ledger logic on Cassandra with "it is usually consistent" assumptions until split replicas disagree in production.
  • Ignoring partitions: Treating a geo-distributed cluster as CA and having no runbook when links fail.
  • Default consistency levels: Shipping with ONE or eventual defaults on paths that need quorum or linearizable reads.
  • CAP on a monolith database: Justifying distributed NoSQL where a single PostgreSQL instance would be simpler and strongly consistent.

Decision checklist

  • Which consistency level does this path need: linearizable, sequential, causal, or eventual?
  • On partition, do we fail closed (CP) or serve possibly stale data (AP)?
  • Does the evaluated store match that behavior under test, not only on the datasheet?
  • For tunable stores, is the read/write level specified per operation in code and docs?
  • Is this data financial, safety-critical, or inventory that cannot tolerate divergence?
  • Have we read PACELC for latency when the network is healthy?
  • Have we chaos-tested partitions and measured what clients actually see?

Example use cases

  • Illustrative scenario — At Meridian Commerce, Payment runs on PostgreSQL with synchronous replication — stale balance reads during partition are unacceptable; brief write unavailability on the primary is acceptable (CP).
  • Illustrative scenario — Meridian’s shopping cart store is AP — line items may flicker across replicas during a network blip, but buyers can keep editing the cart instead of seeing hard errors.
  • Illustrative scenario — Catalog browse tolerates eventually consistent product tiles at the CDN edge; checkout re-validates price and stock before capture so AP browse does not leak into payment correctness.
  • Illustrative scenario — Inventory holds during checkout use quorum-backed reads in the OLTP path — Meridian treats that step as CP even when product browse stays AP.
  • Illustrative scenario — Checkout feature-flag configuration in ZooKeeper is CP — split-brain config would route traffic to half-upgraded pods, so isolated minorities refuse writes rather than diverge.

Related patterns

  • PACELC Theorem — extends CAP with latency-versus-consistency choices when the network is healthy.
  • Eventual Consistency — what AP designs promise users after partitions heal and replicas converge.
  • Quality Attributes — consistency and availability remain among the highest-stakes attribute conflicts in distributed design.
