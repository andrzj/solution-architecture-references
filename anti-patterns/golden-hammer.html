Golden Hammer

Last updated: January 2025

What it is

Golden Hammer is the habit of solving every new problem with a tool the team already trusts, even when the problem’s shape does not fit that tool. Familiarity feels like pragmatism: one stack to hire for, one on-call rotation, one vendor relationship. The anti-pattern appears when that reflex overrides explicit comparison with purpose-built options.

In a logistics-style dispatch domain, a stream broker often earns its place on the event path: assignment and delivery facts decouple yard operations from billing and analytics, as described in Event-Driven Architecture. Golden Hammer appears when that success morphs into an everything-on-the-bus mandate — ephemeral session state, operational email triggers, nightly CSV exports, and ad hoc search over free-text notes routed through the same cluster.

Each choice was defensible in isolation. Collectively they turned Kafka into a general-purpose datastore and job scheduler, complete with consumer lag firefights and compaction topics that were never designed for query patterns. The fix is not to abandon Kafka where it shines, but to match tool to access pattern before reflex kicks in — the same discipline Refactoring Guru describes for the Golden hammer (anti-pattern).

Why it emerges

Teams that have already funded broker expertise, schema governance, and on-call runbooks face sunk cost and organizational convenience: reusing that investment feels cheaper than onboarding a cache, a managed queue, or a search engine for a one-off requirement.

Platform standards amplify the effect. When architecture review treats “everything async” as the default moral choice, engineers frame synchronous HTTP or a relational cache as regression even for request–response paths that need an answer in the same call.

Vendor breadth matters too. A cloud or broker vendor’s “platform” story rewards consolidating spend on one ecosystem. That is rational until proprietary ergonomics stretch a hammer into domains where another tool would reduce toil — a pattern that often foreshadows Vendor Lock-in.

How it manifests

  • A session service writes portal tokens to a compacted topic because the platform team already operates the cluster, although a TTL cache would have matched the access pattern.
  • Batch reconciliations ran as long-lived consumers with hand-rolled retry files instead of a scheduler or warehouse-native SQL, because “we already have Kafka.”
  • Architecture reviews concluded that another topic plus connector could substitute for search or workflow engines without benchmarking latency or operational cost.
  • Custom glue — cron-driven replay jobs, bespoke DLQs, application-side deduplication — compensated for missing broker primitives on the wrong workload.
  • Incidents were explained as “we need more partitions” rather than “this workload should not live on the bus.”

How to recognize it early

  • Workaround language: When the design discussion jumps immediately to “we’ll poll the topic” or “we’ll compact another log,” the team may be fitting Kafka (or any incumbent) to the wrong shape.
  • Missing fitness criteria: Absent written boundaries for when the bus is mandatory versus when a database, cache, or RPC path is acceptable, every proposal sounds like a variation of the same hammer.
  • Expertise monoculture: If no reviewer can describe a credible alternative without outsourcing, familiarity—not requirements—may be driving the call.
  • Disproportionate ops load: A component whose Kafka footprint (retention, ACLs, consumer groups) dwarfs its business value is often a Golden Hammer deployment.

Consequences and remediation

Consequences

  • Performance ceilings: Broker-backed “queues” and “caches” on hot paths add end-to-end latency that purpose-built components would have avoided.
  • Operational debt: Teams reimplemented visibility timeouts, idempotent consumers, and poison-message handling in application code — primitives managed brokers provide natively.
  • Missed ecosystems: Search, scheduling, and caching products bring connectors, SLOs, and community playbooks you do not get by bending one incumbent tool.
  • Attraction risk: Engineers who specialize in stream processing rarely want to maintain cron-driven topic janitors for batch CSV exports.

Remediation strategies

  • Technology fitness functions: Document access pattern, consistency, and retention requirements; state when Kafka is required and when it is optional.
  • ADRs with alternatives: Record where the bus remains the right fit and where a new cache or scheduler was not shoehorned into a topic.
  • Benchmark spikes: For latency-sensitive paths, compare a thin spike on the incumbent hammer against one purpose-built candidate before standardizing.
  • Incremental extraction: Wrap topic-based shortcuts behind ports; swap adapters to Redis, OpenSearch, or a managed queue without rewriting domain logic.

Detailed analysis

Event buses remain the right default for fan-out domain events. Golden Hammer begins when unrelated teams publish directly to shared clusters without domain ownership or review against architecture principles.

Relational Golden Hammer — using Postgres LISTEN/NOTIFY or polling tables as queues — is the structurally identical mistake with different branding. The neutral diagram above lists Postgres because the failure mode is the same: one engine asked to be cache, bus, search, and time-series store at once.

Symmetric over-application happens when a team that lives in Elasticsearch or Kubernetes routes every workload through that stack. The antidote is unchanged: lead with problem characteristics, then shortlist tools — including ones you already operate — on evidence rather than affection.

Escalation patterns

  • Good-enough trap: Low-volume Kafka misuses work in staging; production scale exposes consumer lag and retention cost long after the architectural window to choose a better fit closed.
  • Wrapper proliferation: Abstraction layers that hide topic semantics become load-bearing; migrating off the hammer means rewriting those wrappers too.
  • Certification gravity: Hiring and promotion reward deep Kafka skill, shrinking the bench that can evaluate simpler options credibly.
  • Vendor encouragement: Broker vendors sell “streaming platforms”; their incentives align with broad topic adoption even when a lighter integration would serve the workload better.

Diagnostic checklist

  • Were alternatives evaluated on this component’s access pattern, or was Kafka assumed before requirements were written?
  • Is there an ADR or benchmark comparing the incumbent tool to a purpose-built option for this workload?
  • Does operating this component require reimplementing broker or cache features in application code?
  • If you greenfielded this service today, would the same technology choice survive a blank-slate review?
  • Has one hammer been applied to three or more distinct problem classes where different tools might fit better?

Real-world examples

  • Illustrative scenario — Northwind everything-on-Kafka: At Northwind Logistics, dispatch success on Apache Kafka led to an everything-on-Kafka phase: session state, email fan-out, and finance batch jobs each became topics. Consumer lag during month-end and operational toil on compacted “cache” topics forced a deliberate split: domain events stay on Kafka; TTL state and schedules moved to fit-for-purpose services.
  • Illustrative scenario — Northwind dispatch (appropriate Kafka): LoadAssigned and ShipmentDelivered still fan out to billing and driver notification without synchronous coupling — the original event-driven win preserved while peripheral misuses were retired.

Related anti-patterns

  • Vendor Lock-in – often the result of Golden Hammer applied to vendor products
  • Resume-Driven Development – the inverse: choosing exotic tools for novelty rather than fitness
