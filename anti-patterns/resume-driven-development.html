Resume-Driven Development

Last updated: January 2025

What it is

Resume-driven development (RDD) is choosing technology to pad individual credentials instead of matching system requirements. The tell is that the stack cannot be defended with load, compliance, or team skills — only with “industry is moving here” or “great learning opportunity.”

Learning new tools is healthy. The anti-pattern is when career optics override organizational duty: the organization pays operational tax while advocates collect another production keyword.

A familiar shape is steering a straightforward dispatch or assignment domain from Postgres toward a managed graph store when create, reassign, and complete flows need only relational invariants — a graph query language replaces SQL the team already knows, without multi-hop analytics in the requirements.

Why it emerges

Job markets reward production keywords — Kubernetes, Kafka, graph databases — more than years on a stable relational stack. Sponsors who lack production experience with the new tool still expect the organization to fund the learning curve.

Weak architecture governance lets a loud advocate skip fitness review. Event streaming may be justified elsewhere, but the data store choice closes in one meeting without a “considered options” table in docs/decisions/.

Hype cycles supply fresh targets each conference season. Graph-database pitches blur the line between fraud-ring detection (legitimate graph work) and tabular CRUD with extra hops.

How it manifests

  • Dispatch justifies a graph store with “web-scale routing” while measured load is thousands of assignment updates per day.
  • Design docs name hyperscaler patterns without mapping constraints to the actual hub-and-spoke or regional topology.
  • After the sponsor leaves, on-call cannot explain traversal plans or backup restore for the new data tier.
  • Incident reviews blame “driver quirks” on timeouts that Postgres handled for years.
  • New hires ask why a three-table CRUD domain needs a dozen microservices and four data engines.

How to recognize it early

  • “Cool” without a requirement: “State of the art” or “resume builder for the team” with no latency, compliance, or query-shape justification.
  • Scale mismatch: Operational surface sized for 100× current measured volume.
  • Single champion: One engineer owns the advocacy and the only prior experience with the tool.
  • Chasing releases: Stack churns to newest frameworks while the existing monolith and Postgres still meet SLOs.

Consequences and remediation

Consequences

  • Ops tax: Graph backups, driver upgrades, and explain plans for assignments that were a handful of SQL statements.
  • Hiring mismatch: Job posts demand graph + mesh + streaming depth; candidates optimize for buzzwords, not domain fit.
  • Bus factor: The sponsor understood migration scripts; the remaining squad inherited a black box.
  • Credibility hit: Finance sees cluster cost against load volumes that fit a single managed database instance.

Remediation strategies

  • Fitness review: Require measurable criteria for new platforms — graph stores only when path-tracing queries dominate measured read traffic — not conference slides.
  • Technology radar: Place exotic engines in Trial for proven graph-shaped workloads; keep OLTP on Adopt relational stores until an ADR proves otherwise.
  • Boring by default: Spend Dan McKinley-style innovation tokens on real differentiators, not CRUD storage fashion.
  • Learn outside prod: Hackathons and sandbox clusters satisfy résumé curiosity without coupling production uptime to day-one expertise.

Detailed analysis

RDD is misaligned incentives: individuals gain portfolio lines; the organization gains pager load. Long-term fix is rewarding reliability, cost, and delivery — not stack exoticism.

Modernization is legitimate when EOL databases, hiring markets, or compliance force change. A graph pivot is not that when Postgres still meets latency, auditors are satisfied, and DBAs are on call.

Innovation tokens help platform leads say no: spend one on graph when investigations need variable-depth traversals; do not spend one on listing today’s open assignments by hub.

Escalation patterns

  • Technology sprawl: Dispatch, billing, and notify each add a fashionable store; nobody owns end-to-end restore drills.
  • Abandonment: Sponsor leaves; the team maintains ETL and query scripts nobody authored.
  • Complexity accumulation: Immature graph or mesh ops require custom health checks and one-off migration tooling.
  • Cargo cult lock-in: New hires learn only the exotic path; the next feature defaults to it because it is familiar.

Diagnostic checklist

  • Can you name a requirement that Postgres or the existing monolith cannot meet — with evidence?
  • Is the tool adopted because the team already knows it, or because one person wants it on LinkedIn?
  • Would this choice survive a post-mortem if dispatch failed during peak season?
  • Does the whole squad endorse it, or one vocal sponsor?
  • Was a boring alternative rejected for reasons other than insufficient capability?
  • Who operates this stack in year three, and at what runbook cost?

Real-world examples

  • Illustrative scenario — Northwind graph for CRUD: At Northwind Logistics, FieldOps load assign/complete/reassign moved to a managed graph store; eight months later a strangler migrated back to Postgres after the architect exited — zero traversal queries in production metrics.
  • Illustrative scenario — Northwind mesh on a monolith boundary: Istio landed on three nodes for an internal API still deployed as one FieldOps artifact; ops burned significant time on certificates while request volume stayed flat.
  • Illustrative scenario — Northwind Kafka everywhere: Event-sourced every state change “for audit,” though compliance only required append-only shipment logs in SQL — consumers lagged, dispatch UI read stale assignment state.

Related anti-patterns

  • Golden Hammer – the inverse: using familiar technology instead of learning new things; RDD and Golden Hammer pull in opposite directions
  • Magic Pushbutton (BDUF) – RDD often manifests during BDUF phases where there is time to design elaborate architectures without implementation feedback
  • Vendor Lock-in – RDD with a specific vendor's proprietary stack creates deep lock-in that the organization later must fund to unwind
