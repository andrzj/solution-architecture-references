Microservices Architecture

Independently deployable services, each aligned to a bounded business capability, communicating over a network with their own data stores.

⏱ 10 min read

What it is

Microservices are independently deployable services, each owning its data and exposing a narrow business capability over the network. At Meridian Commerce, Catalog (product discovery), Order (checkout and carts), and Payment (capture and refunds) ship as separate deployables with separate databases — “micro” refers to scope, not line count.

Meridian’s Catalog team can roll out search ranking without waiting on Payment’s card-network certification window, because each service has its own pipeline and schema.

Why it exists

When many teams share one monolith, releases queue, failures spread, and scaling means copying code paths that do not need the extra capacity. Microservices align ownership with Conway’s Law: the communication structure and the runtime structure match so teams can ship in parallel.

Meridian split after modular-monolith CI still could not unblock Payment during peak checkout. The trade is operational depth — gateways, brokers, tracing, and on-call per service — so the architecture pays off only when autonomy and isolation are real requirements, not aspirational labels.

When to use

  • Catalog, Order, and Payment teams must deploy on different schedules without a shared Meridian release train.
  • Catalog search, checkout APIs, and Payment workers need different scale profiles and cost controls.
  • Meridian already runs container platforms, per-service CI, centralized logging, and distributed tracing in production.
  • Bounded contexts for browse, checkout, and settlement are stable enough that API churn is rare.
  • Payment must use a regulated runtime or datastore the storefront stack cannot host inside one process.

When not to use

  • One small team still owns most of Meridian’s codebase and spends more time on infra tickets than product work.
  • Checkout rules change weekly and service contracts would churn faster than the domain understanding.
  • Meridian lacks automated deploys, health checks, and tracing — outages will be harder to diagnose than inside a monolith.
  • Checkout requires synchronous, all-or-nothing consistency across every step without a designed saga or outbox pattern.

Typical architecture

Meridian storefront clients hit an API gateway for auth and routing. Catalog, Order, and Payment answer through REST or gRPC; checkout side effects also flow across a message broker. Each service keeps a private database; a service mesh adds mTLS and retries between pods. The diagram labels the three retail paths as Order, User (shopper account and session), and Payment — Meridian maps User to account concerns tied to Catalog and Order, while Catalog owns product data.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Payment can certify card changes without freezing Catalog’s weekly merchandising deploys.
  • A Catalog outage can degrade browse while Order and Payment stay up if clients handle partial failure.
  • Meridian scales search replicas without paying for duplicate Payment workers on every node.
  • Payment can adopt a PCI-friendly stack while Catalog stays on the storefront framework.
  • Each team’s repo stays small enough for on-call engineers to reason about during incidents.

Cons

  • Meridian operates gateways, brokers, meshes, and many pipelines instead of one FieldOps-style artifact.
  • Every checkout path pays network latency and faces timeouts when dependencies blip.
  • Cart plus payment capture needs sagas, idempotency keys, or outbox patterns instead of one DB transaction.
  • Local reproduction requires containers or stubs for Catalog, Order, Payment, and the broker together.
  • Renaming a field on Order’s API forces coordinated releases with Payment consumers.

Implementation notes

Cut Meridian services on business seams — Catalog, Order, Payment — not on generic “data” or “auth” layers. Never share PostgreSQL schemas between services; publish data through APIs or events. Give each service its own CI job, health checks, and rollback. Export OpenTelemetry traces from day one so on-call can follow a checkout ID across hops. Put authentication, rate limits, and routing in the gateway; put mTLS and retries in the mesh instead of copying libraries into every repo.

Consumer-driven contract tests (for example Pact) let Payment verify Catalog and Order do not ship breaking JSON silently.

Common failure modes

  • Shared database: Order and Payment both write checkout tables; schema migrations reintroduce monolith coupling with extra latency.
  • Chatty checkout: Placing one cart fans out through many synchronous RPCs, so one slow dependency abandons carts.
  • Distributed monolith: Services deploy separately but share fat client libraries and mandatory upgrade days.
  • Blind production: Without trace IDs, Meridian cannot tell whether Payment or Order caused elevated 500s.
  • Technical slicing: A “database service” forces every feature through extra hops instead of Catalog owning catalog reads.

Decision checklist

  • Does each Meridian team own build, deploy, and on-call for its service end to end?
  • Is there a working per-service pipeline with automated promotion and rollback?
  • Are traces and structured logs searchable before exposing checkout on the new topology?
  • Does every service have a dedicated datastore with no cross-service SQL?
  • Do service lines match stable bounded contexts (catalog browse, order taking, payment settlement)?
  • Do consumer contracts gate merges that would break Catalog, Order, or Payment clients?

Example use cases

  • Meridian Commerce split: Catalog, Order, and Payment services let merchandising, checkout, and finance teams ship and scale on independent cadences.
  • Netflix streaming platform: Many small services replaced a DVD-era monolith so teams could deploy and fail independently across AWS regions.
  • Amazon retail internals: Service APIs between teams became the pattern later productized as AWS primitives.

Related patterns

  • Modular Monolith — The recommended intermediate step before microservices; cheaper boundary changes, same design discipline.
  • Event-Driven Architecture — Frequently combined with microservices for async inter-service communication.
  • Service-Oriented Architecture — Microservices' predecessor; coarser-grained, ESB-centric, enterprise-focused.
